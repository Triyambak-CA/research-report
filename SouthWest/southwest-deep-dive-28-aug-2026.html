India's largest organised private exploratory-drilling contractor - 43 rigs, 3.3 million metres drilled, a record Rs 761 crore order book and the highest return on capital in its peer set. It is also a company whose profit does not convert to cash, whose reported margin expansion turns out to be the exit of a near-zero-margin trading line, and which is preparing to spend its entire net worth on a coal block it has not yet been licensed to mine. This note presents the evidence and a two-way pitch. It issues no rating.

Top 3 Positives

  • The demand is verified by government data, not management. Coal India's own technical arm plans to outsource the majority of its detailed drilling (5.80 of 10.00 lakh metres), and the Ministry of Coal has committed Rs 5,925 Cr and 55.50 lakh metres over five years - roughly 2.8x this company's entire annual metreage, every year.
  • Real operating delivery and the best returns in the peer set. A record 4 lakh metres drilled in FY26, 165 projects completed, 3.3 million metres with zero lost-time injury, ~30% of India's hydraulic exploration rig fleet, and a 20% ROCE that leads every listed comparable. Incremental return on drilling capex clears the cost of equity even on pessimistic assumptions.
  • Insulated from the funding failure that wrecks the sector narrative. 77% of the order book is private - Reliance, Hindustan Zinc, Vedanta - so the documented collapse in government exploration disbursement lands on the story rather than on the next two years of revenue.

Top 3 Risks

  • The margin never actually expanded. Restated for the exit of a ~1%-margin coal-trading line, core operating margin went from about 24.6% to 24.0% in FY26 - it fell. The word "trading" appears zero times across all three earnings-call transcripts.
  • Profit does not become cash, and a Rs 200 Cr bill is coming. FY26 earned Rs 30.8 Cr and collected Rs 12.0 Cr; receivables absorbed more than the entire year's profit while the credit-loss charge fell. Free cash flow was minus Rs 35.6 Cr and net debt nearly tripled. Coal-block Phase I needs Rs 200 Cr against a Rs 202 Cr net worth and an Rs 83.69 Cr bank limit unchanged since 2024.
  • The order book is not what it says. Rs 761 Cr is quoted including GST against revenue booked net of it, includes an award granted a week after the balance date that management denied on the call, and Rs 90 Cr of it vanished in one quarter unexplained. Meanwhile promoters took Rs 14.7 Cr net out of the market using warrants they issued to themselves.

"This is a bet on India's largest private driller converting a record order book and a 266-hectare coal block into owned reserves - priced as if today's peak-cycle margin is permanent and the Rs 200 crore to get there is already in hand - and it breaks if the margin mean-reverts before the mine is funded."

SECTION 01

Industry & Sector Analysis

South West Pinnacle sells metres drilled into the ground. Its market is India's mineral and coal exploration programme - a business where the announced money and the disbursed money have diverged sharply, and where the company's insulation from that gap is the most under-appreciated fact in the file.

What the industry is. Exploration drilling is the first step of the mining value chain. Before a block can be auctioned, financed or mined, somebody must drill core samples, log them geophysically and produce a geological report that a ministry will approve. In India that work is done by three kinds of organisation: the state agencies (Geological Survey of India, and MECL, the exploration CPSE), the captive technical arms of the large miners (chiefly CMPDI inside Coal India), and private contractors, of which South West Pinnacle is the largest organised player.

Size, and why a clean number does not exist. There is no published figure for India's total annual exploration spend across all three channels, and this note will not invent one. The hard anchors are these. MECL, the state exploration CPSE, earned Rs 406 cr of revenue in FY25 and drilled 4,51,539 metres, up 29.5% on FY24. The Ministry of Coal has earmarked Rs 5,925 cr for coal and lignite exploration over FY2026-27 to FY2030-31, covering about 1,845 sq km and a drilling programme of 55.50 lakh metres. Globally, S&P Global put the 2025 non-ferrous exploration budget at USD 12.40 bn, the third consecutive year of nominal decline, with grassroots exploration at a record-low 21% share. India's specific share of that global budget could not be verified - the source report is paywalled, and no substitute figure is offered here.

Set the coal programme against the company: 55.50 lakh metres over five years is 11.1 lakh metres a year. South West Pinnacle drilled roughly 4 lakh metres in FY26. The government's coal exploration programme alone is therefore about 2.8 times the company's entire current annual metreage. Scarcity of demand is not this company's problem.

The Cycle Position Is Contested By The Data Itself

Management describes an exploration upcycle running "the next three to five years". The physical demand data supports that. The government disbursement data does not. Both are true at once, and Section 20 sets them side by side. The resolution matters because it decides whether the current 24-27% margin is a cycle peak or a new floor - and management itself calls the business "quite cyclical".

Where the cycle actually stands. On physical activity, India is mid-upcycle. Coal production crossed 1 billion tonnes, with captive and commercial mines alone contributing 210.46 MT in FY25-26, above 200 MT for the first time. 141 commercial coal mines have been auctioned cumulatively. Coal India's assessed geological resource stood at 389.42 bn tonnes as at 01-Apr-2024, and converting resource into mineable reserve is drilling work. On the critical-minerals side the policy architecture is new and the activity is thin: the first-ever Exploration Licence auction launched only on 13-Mar-2025 for 13 blocks across 10 states, preferred bidders emerged for 7, and several critical-mineral auctions since 2023 have been cancelled outright for want of qualified bidders.

One quiet counter-indicator deserves airtime. IBEF records that the number of reporting mines in India fell from 2,046 in FY24 to 1,973 in FY25 (metallic mines 799 to 772). Exploration precedes mine-count growth by years, so a falling count is not proof of decline - but it does sit awkwardly beside any claim that a mining boom is already under way.

Structural shifts that matter to a contractor. Three are real. First, the MMDR Amendment Act 2023 created the Exploration Licence, opening reconnaissance and prospecting for 29 critical and deep-seated minerals to private parties - this is what allows a services contractor to imagine one day owning discoveries rather than billing for metres. Second, import substitution in critical minerals became national policy with the National Critical Mineral Mission (approved 29-Jan-2025). Third, and most concretely, Coal India's own captive arm now plans to outsource the majority of its detailed drilling - see Section 3. That single procurement decision is worth more to this company than the entire critical-minerals narrative.

Who captures value across the chain. Very little of it sits with the driller. The mine owner captures the resource rent; the mine developer-cum-operator captures the production economics; the exploration contractor captures a fee per metre on a fixed-term contract, and carries the receivable while it waits. That is precisely why the company is trying to move upstream into owning a coal block, and why that move is simultaneously the bull case and the balance-sheet risk (Sections 14 and 17). The margin structure is unusual for a contractor - 24-27% EBITDA - and the honest explanation is scarcity of qualified rigs and crews rather than any pricing power over the client.

Regulatory architecture. Entry is gated by technical qualification rather than licensing: government tenders (ONGC, CMPDI, CGWB) require prior-experience and equipment thresholds and then run to lowest bidder, while private clients (Reliance, Hindustan Zinc, Vedanta) invite quotations from a pre-qualified set and weigh on-ground execution. There is no price control, no import duty protection and no quality control order shielding this industry. The barrier is capital and crews, not paperwork - which cuts both ways, since it also means no regulatory moat protects the incumbent.

SECTION 02

Business Model & Value Chain

The company owns 43 drilling rigs and the crews to run them, and rents that capability out across six end-markets. It is paid per metre, on contracts of six months to four years, by clients who are overwhelmingly private. It is now trying to become the owner of what it drills.

Company-level, in one paragraph. South West Pinnacle Exploration buys rigs, drill rods, bits, mud and diesel, hires geoscientists and drilling crews, mobilises them to a client's block, drills core holes to a specified depth, logs them geophysically, and hands back samples plus a geological report. It bills per metre drilled or per borehole completed. Over 19 years it has drilled 3.3 million metres with no lost-time injury and completed more than 165 projects. As of Q1 FY27 it was running 20 projects across 8 states with a Rs 761 cr order book.

Two things a first read misses
  • The JV profit brings in no cash. Share of JV profit was Rs 1.32 cr in Q1 FY27 and about Rs 1.5 cr for all of last year, and it lifts consolidated PAT above standalone PAT. But the finance head confirmed "that money has yet to be repatriated" and the MD confirmed "the dividend stage has not come yet". The company has also lent money to the JV and is recovering that loan ahead of any dividend. Cash has flowed out to Oman, not in from it.
  • The promoter sits on both sides. The company disclosed the rights-issue subscription as a related-party transaction at arm's length. Section 7 takes this up.
Unknown unknowns across the whole business
  • Utilisation is above 100%, and the excess is bought in. Asked for resource utilisation in Q1 FY27, management answered "More than 100%", explaining that work has been outsourced to other contractors and rigs hired in. Incremental revenue is therefore being delivered on third-party capacity at third-party economics. If the outsourced mix keeps rising, the 24-27% margin should compress - and no disclosure exists that would let an outsider track it.
  • The client owns the relationship, not the company. Every rupee of the order book is a fixed-term contract. There is no annuity, no installed base, no aftermarket. The Reliance extension is exercisable at Reliance's option.
  • Working capital is the real business model. Debtor days are 175 and the cash conversion cycle is 452 days. The company finances its clients. Growth consumes cash by construction, which is why FY26 free cash flow was negative Rs 33 cr in a record profit year.
  • Diesel and mobilisation costs are unhedged and unpassed. No fuel escalation clause is disclosed in any public document.

SECTION 04

Competitive Advantage: The 6 Cs

The durable advantage here is narrow and it is one thing: a fleet and a crew base at a scale nobody else in the private sector has assembled, in a business where capital and trained people are the binding constraint. Everything else is weak.

CostWeak

No structural input advantage. Diesel, rods and bits are commodity purchases at market prices, and there is no disclosed fuel escalation clause. The 24-27% margin comes from scarcity pricing in a tight rig market, not from a cost position. The tell is that incremental work is now being subcontracted and delivered on hired rigs at above-100% utilisation - a company with a real cost edge does not rent its competitor's capacity to fill an order book.

CapacityStrong

The one genuine moat. 43 rigs, up from 40 at FY26 close, with more on order, plus geophysical logging and seismic equipment and crews trained across coal, mineral, CBM and hydrogeology work. Rigs are fungible across commodities, so the fleet redeploys when a tender stream dries up. Replicating this needs years of capital and, harder, trained drillers. Note the fleet fell from 41 to 35 between FY22 and FY24 before rebuilding to 43 - the capacity was allowed to shrink through the last soft patch, which says something about how the cycle bites.

CapitalWeak

A Rs 682 cr company with Rs 80 cr of debt, a BBB-band credit rating, Rs 12 cr of FY26 operating cash flow and no dividend, facing a Rs 200 cr Phase-I commitment on the coal block plus open-ended Oman exploration spend. The balance sheet is not a weapon here; it is the constraint. Access to capital is ordinary and the cost of it is a small-cap cost.

ChannelNone

There is no channel. Work is won contract by contract, through tenders in the government segment and invited quotations in the private segment. No distribution, no dealer network, no installed base, no aftermarket revenue. Every rupee must be re-won when a contract ends.

Customer captivityModerate

Genuinely two-sided. Switching costs are real mid-project - a client will not remobilise a new contractor onto a half-drilled block - and long relationships won the Rs 307 cr Hindustan Zinc award without a pure price fight. But captivity runs the wrong way at the portfolio level: about 60% of revenue sits in two contracts and Reliance alone is 35-40%. That is the company being captive to its customers, not the reverse.

ComplianceWeak

ISO 9001:2015 certification and technical pre-qualification for ONGC, Oil India and CMPDI tenders raise the bar modestly, and the Oil India seismic empanelment won in Q1 FY27 is a real credential. But there is no licence, no statutory approval and no quality control order that keeps a competitor out. The 3.3 million metres with zero lost-time injury is a genuine credential in a safety-screened tender - and it is a reputation, which is lost in one accident.

The single most important moat source, and its half-life. It is capacity - specifically the combination of an owned rig fleet and trained crews at a scale no other private Indian contractor has. Its half-life is best estimated at three to five years, and the constraint is not capital but people: rigs can be bought within a year by anyone with funding, while drill crews and geoscientists with a decade of Indian field experience cannot. The moat erodes fastest in exactly the scenario the bulls want, because a sustained upcycle at 24-27% margins is precisely what funds a competitor's fleet. The company's own decision to subcontract and hire rigs at above-100% utilisation is the first visible crack: it is currently teaching third-party capacity how to serve its clients.

The Margin Did Not Expand - The Denominator Shrank

CRISIL's rating rationale of 05-Dec-2025 records that FY25's reported margin was "weighed down by minimal margin of ~1% in coal trading - barring this business, the operating margin was 24.6%", on about Rs 46 cr of trading revenue. Its 10-Jul-2026 rationale confirms the company "did not undertake any low-margin coal trading in fiscal 2026". FY26 reported margin: 24.0%. Like for like, the core services margin went from about 24.6% to 24.0% - it fell 0.6 points. The reported jump from 18.9% to 24.0% is largely the arithmetic of removing a fifth of the revenue base that carried almost no profit. The word "trading" appears zero times across all three earnings-call transcripts. Any model extrapolating the margin path forward is extrapolating an accounting artefact: there is no second trading business to exit.

Restated properly, FY26 remains an excellent year - services revenue up about 81% on a flat core margin. That is a volume story, and volume stories in this business are cyclical. Management supplies the bear reading itself: "There is a huge shortage of resources currently in the market. So, the margins are great right now" and "our business is more cyclical, if you understand." A second piece of evidence sits against durability: utilisation runs "more than 100%", achieved by outsourcing work to other contractors and hiring rigs in, with more than ten rigs subcontracted in as at May-2026. FY26 delivered record revenue, record profit and the best margin in a decade - and cash from operations fell from Rs 27 cr to Rs 12 cr. Incremental returns. Between FY23 and FY26, EBIT rose from about Rs 13 cr to about Rs 47 cr, an increase of Rs 34 cr. Capital employed rose from about Rs 179 cr to about Rs 284 cr, an increase of Rs 105 cr. Incremental return on the capital deployed over that window is therefore roughly 32% - genuinely good, and the strongest single number in the financial file. The caveat is that a large part of the EBIT increase came from margin expansion rather than from the new assets themselves, so this figure flatters the rigs and flatters the cycle together.

Working capital. Debtor days deteriorated from 155 to 175 in FY26 even as the client mix shifted toward private counterparties, which is the wrong direction and the wrong mix to explain it. Days payable stretched from 152 to 232 - the company is funding itself partly on its suppliers. Inventory days rose from 376 to 508. The cash conversion cycle at 452 days means capital turns over less than once a year. On a Rs 243 cr revenue base, every incremental rupee of sales requires a substantial rupee of working capital, and that is the arithmetic behind negative free cash flow in a record year.

Balance sheet. Borrowings rose from Rs 65 cr to Rs 80 cr; debt-to-equity is 0.39 against net worth of about Rs 204 cr. On the Q1 FY27 call the finance head answered a question on net debt with "it is around INR 15 crores at the moment with a very good debt-equity of less than 0.39" - two figures that cannot both be right, since Rs 15 cr against Rs 204 cr of equity is a ratio near 0.07. The Rs 15 cr is most plausibly net debt or a single facility. It is a small thing, but a chief financial officer quoting two mutually inconsistent numbers in one sentence is worth noting. CWIP of Rs 15 cr at Mar-2025 went to zero at Mar-2026 while fixed assets jumped from Rs 67 cr to Rs 115 cr, so the capital work in progress did convert into productive assets - a clean result on a check that often fails.

This is not a fraud profile. It is the profile of a fast-growing company whose finance function and disclosure have not kept pace with its balance sheet. The cash is real; the receivable is the problem; and the assurance spend on the whole enterprise is roughly Rs 5 lakh a year.

2. Does the cash exist? Yes.

Interest received against average cash and deposits yields 8.14% (FY24), 4.19% (FY25) and 6.60% (FY26). The low FY25 figure is fully explained by timing: Rs 25.48 cr of preferential-issue money and Rs 9.32 cr of warrant money landed on 21-Feb-2025, five weeks before the year end. The cash is real. This check passes cleanly and deserves to be said plainly.

It also destroys the FY25 balance-sheet headline. The company's own capital-management note shows net debt of Rs 81.97 cr (FY24), Rs 23.10 cr (FY25) and Rs 67.30 cr (FY26), with gearing of 65.9%, 13.4% and 33.2%. The celebrated FY25 gearing was a point-in-time artefact of a February equity raise plus Rs 11.51 cr of asset disposals. Within twelve months net debt was back up 191% - in the same period management told the market it was "getting towards being a debt-free company".

3. Capital work in progress and capitalised exploration

Tangible CWIP converts properly - Rs 15 cr at Mar-2025 went to zero at Mar-2026 as fixed assets rose from Rs 67 cr to Rs 115 cr. The problem is the intangible line. "Intangible assets under development - exploration and evaluation assets" has grown four years running to Rs 19.31 cr, and the FY25 annual report contains no accounting policy at all for intangible assets, for exploration and evaluation assets, for Ind AS 106, or for their impairment. The policy section covers property, investment property, leases, inventories and financial instruments, and simply omits this. No capitalisation criterion, no impairment trigger, no amortisation basis is stated. Ind AS 23 requires disclosure of borrowing cost capitalised; the policy says qualifying-asset borrowing costs are capitalised but no amount is disclosed in any year, so whether interest or employee cost sits inside that Rs 19.31 cr is unknowable from public documents. At 9.5% of net worth, generating nil revenue and nil segment result, this is the least-governed asset on the balance sheet.

5. Tax - normal accrual, deferred cash

6. Contingent liabilities and the commitment nobody mentioned

Contingent liabilities were Rs 14.68 cr (FY23), Rs 22.65 cr (FY24) and Rs 24.67 cr (FY25) - the last equal to 14.4% of net worth, mostly bank guarantees of Rs 22.54 cr. The overlooked item is capital commitments, which went from nil at 31-Mar-2024 to Rs 24.98 cr at 31-Mar-2025 and appear in no investor communication - though they were the honest leading indicator of FY26's Rs 42.54 cr of capex. FY26 contingent liabilities are not disclosed.

7. Related-party transactions

Two patterns matter. First, a revolving loan: Rs 9.50 cr went out to enterprises influenced by the promoters in FY25 and Rs 9.50 cr came back; Rs 14.55 cr out and Rs 14.61 cr back in FY24. Interest earned was Rs 3.30 lakh and Rs 6.12 lakh respectively, which at normal rates implies very short tenors - so this is not a case of under-charged interest, and it should not be presented as one. The finding is the pattern: a company that borrows at 7.8-9.8% and runs a cash cycle beyond 240 days is cycling Rs 9-15 cr a year of gross treasury flow through promoter-influenced entities, and the counterparties are never named against amounts. The named related enterprises are Chandra Prabhu International Ltd, Thakurji Towers Pvt Ltd, Ravmo Imports LLP, and the two promoter HUFs.

Second, and more important, the FY25 pivot to related-party subcontracting. The "cost of sub-contract work and technical fees" line went from Rs 3.31 cr (FY24) to Rs 25.39 cr (FY25), up 667% - and Rs 12.41 cr of it, 48.9%, was purchased from subsidiaries and a joint venture, against nil in both prior years. FY26's equivalent line is Rs 104.07 cr with no breakup published. Set against it: employee cost fell from 20.6% to 14.7% of service revenue while other operating expense rose from 29.8% to 42.8%. The cost structure moved 13 points away from own labour and toward bought-in capacity in the very year the "43 owned rigs" moat was supposedly being monetised. That inference rests on ratios, because the FY26 note that would settle it does not exist yet.

8. Auditor and assurance

Statutory auditor is Doogar & Associates (FRN 000561N), re-appointed at the 18th AGM on 27-Sep-2024 for five years. Opinions are unmodified in every year, CARO is clean, there are no emphasis-of-matter paragraphs and no auditor resignation. The single key audit matter in both FY24 and FY25 is revenue recognition - specifically the complexity of identifying stage of completion, which is management-estimated.

Against that, the audit fee is Rs 5.00 lakh, unchanged across FY24 and FY25 while revenue grew 34% - of which Rs 20,000 covers a full year of quarterly limited reviews. That is 0.028% of revenue. And the internal auditor has been a company employee for three consecutive years: an external chartered accountant resigned in May-2024 and was replaced at the same board meeting by the firm's own Manager (Audit and Accounts), reappointed in 2025 and replaced by another employee of the same department in May-2026. Section 138 permits it. But a listed company whose only key audit matter is management-estimated revenue, and whose receivables grew 57%, having its controls reviewed by a member of its own accounts department, is an assurance structure worth naming.

9. Two hard disclosure failures

  • All joint-venture profit is unaudited. The consolidated auditor states that the Group's share of profit from three joint ventures - Rs 146.90 lakh in FY26, Rs 131.91 lakh in FY25 - comes from financial information "not audited by us... furnished to us by the Board of Directors", and opines that these are "not material to the Group". They are 4.4% of consolidated PAT, and consolidated EPS runs 7.2% above standalone largely because of them. The entire Oman story sits inside entities no auditor has examined.
  • The Schedule III ratio note is unreliable. The FY25 report states FY24's net profit ratio as 55.87%; the FY24 report states 5.31% for the same year, and 5.31% is correct. Trade payable turnover for FY24 is given as 10.82 in one report and 5.70 in the other. Two independent errors in one mandatory nine-line note, board-signed and auditor-reviewed, is a pattern rather than a typo.

One further judgement flag: the company holds 80% of South West Samit JV yet equity-accounts it as a joint venture rather than consolidating it. That is permissible only where the contractual arrangement confers joint rather than unilateral control, and no such arrangement is described anywhere in the annual report. The classification has a consequence - Rs 4.45 cr of geological and seismic services bought from that entity in FY25 does not eliminate on consolidation precisely because of it.

10. A subsidiary that cannot pay its own bills

South West Resources Private Limited (formerly Pilot Pipelines), wholly owned, has carried a going-concern material uncertainty in its audit report at both 31-Mar-2025 and 31-Mar-2026: accumulated losses of Rs 3.15 cr have fully eroded its net worth and current liabilities exceed current assets by Rs 2.66 cr. It survives on a letter of financial support from the parent. The parent carries a Rs 5.00 cr loan to it as "unsecured and considered good" with no provision, and CARO records that the parent has never demanded repayment. Together with the Rs 0.42 cr investment, that is Rs 5.42 cr - 2.7% of net worth and 17.6% of FY26 profit - supported by a letter the parent wrote to itself. The position is improving and the amount is small; the disclosure asymmetry is the point, since it appears only in the consolidated auditor's Other Matter paragraph and in no investor material ever.

Earnings quality score

5.5 / 10. Marks earned: the cash is demonstrably real; the tax reconciles cleanly with no structuring; the auditor is stable with unmodified opinions and clean CARO; the CFO and Company Secretary have not changed in three years; promoter pay fell from 23.7% to 14.2% of profit and rose slower than staff pay; and FY26's profit is operating profit, with other income down to 8.7% of PBT and the loss-making coal-trading line exited rather than milked for revenue optics. The promoter flow, netted properly

This is the item most published commentary gets wrong, in both directions. The margin explanation management never gave

FY26 Margin Expansion Is Largely A Mix Artefact

CRISIL's rating rationale of 05-Dec-2025 states that FY25's reported margin was "weighed down by minimal margin of ~1% in coal trading - barring this business, the operating margin was 24.6%", on roughly Rs 46 cr of trading revenue. Its 10-Jul-2026 rationale confirms the company "did not undertake any low-margin coal trading in fiscal 2026". FY26 reported margin was 24.0%. So like for like, the core services margin went from about 24.6% to 24.0% - down 0.6 points, in the year management described as having a "huge shortage of resources" driving pricing.

The word "trading" appears zero times across all three earnings-call transcripts. Asked directly on 06-May-2026 to explain the margin, the Chairman answered with operating leverage: "once you start covering your fixed cost, the profit margin goes abnormally high." He did not mention that roughly a fifth of the prior year's revenue base, carrying almost no margin, had simply been removed from the denominator. Any model extrapolating the 18.9% to 24.0% path forward is extrapolating an accounting artefact - there is no second trading business to exit.

In fairness to management, the trading exit is a real improvement in reported margin, and shareholders own reported margin. The objection is about the future, not the past: the mechanism cannot repeat, and a company that explains a five-point margin move without mentioning that it removed a fifth of its revenue base has not explained the margin move. Restated properly, FY26 was still an excellent year - services revenue up 81% with a flat core margin - which is a volume story, not a pricing story.

SECTION 09

Regulatory & Company-Specific Risks

There is no live regulatory action against this company and no price control over its industry. The risks here are concentration risks - of customer, of counterparty, and of a single unbuilt mine - and they are unusually severe for a company of this size.

Concentration - the dominant risk

Customer. The audited "three major customers" disclosure runs 33.33% (FY23), 42.38% (FY24) and 48.00% (FY25) - rising every year. Management confirmed Reliance Industries at about 35% of FY26 revenue and guided 35-40% for FY27, and stated that two contracts are about 60% of the order book (on the numbers, Reliance plus Hindustan Zinc is closer to 69% of it). The Reliance relationship runs on an extension whose further renewal is at the client's option. A single procurement decision at one counterparty could remove more than a third of the topline. For context, the entire FY26 profit of Rs 33 cr is smaller than the annual revenue at risk from that one client.

Counterparty and receivable. Debtor days of 175 mean roughly Rs 117 cr of revenue sits uncollected at any time against a Rs 204 cr net worth. The mix is now 77% private, which should improve collection - yet debtor days worsened in FY26. That divergence is unexplained in any public document.

Single asset. The Jharkhand coal block is one block, one clearance chain and one commodity. It requires MoC approval of the geological report, then the mining plan, then environmental and forest clearance from the state - three sequential gates, each with no published timeline, before a rupee of the Rs 200 cr Phase-I spend earns anything.

Credit rating

CRISIL rated the company BBB with a Positive outlook and short-term A3+ on 05-Dec-2025, having revised the outlook up from Stable, and issued a further rating action on 10-Jul-2026; on the Q1 FY27 call the Chairman stated the rating had been raised "from BBB to BBB+", which is consistent with the prior Positive outlook. Total rated facilities are Rs 83.69 cr - Rs 41.69 cr of bank guarantees and Rs 42.00 cr of cash credit - and have not been enhanced since October 2024, with fund-based utilisation averaging 77%.

Rating agencies disclose what investor presentations hide, and three points from the rationales matter more than the grade itself:

  • Receivables have remained above 150 days for three consecutive years - stated by CRISIL, and directly contradicting the CFO's "45 to 90 days" answer on the October-2025 call.
  • Revenue depends on tender awards, with order-inflow timing and contract uncertainty named as risks.
  • CRISIL's own downside triggers: operating margin deteriorating below 21-23%, or interest coverage falling below 2.0-2.3x, or a sustained slowdown in revenue growth. Those are the cleanest externally-set thresholds available and they are carried into the monitorables dashboard in Section 15.
  • CRISIL also names funding of the coal-block capex as "a key monitorable", and separately puts first production at fiscal 2029 - a year later than the company's own published presentation.

A BBB+ rating is investment grade but modest. It prices bank debt at a small-cap cost, and the existing Rs 41.69 cr guarantee limit is smaller than the Rs 70-80 cr performance guarantee the coal block alone requires.

Regulatory exposure

  • No SEBI order, investigation or NCLT proceeding was found against the company or its promoters. A High Court matter naming a "Vikas Jain" surfaced in search but could not be attributed to this promoter and is therefore excluded rather than repeated.
  • Revenue-sharing obligation. The Jharkhand block carries a 24.25% revenue share to the government - a first-rupee deduction from gross realisation, not a profit share. Any coal-block valuation must be built after this deduction.
  • Clearance risk. Environmental and forest clearance for a Jharkhand coal block is the single most schedule-uncertain item in the plan. The FY2028-29 production date depends on it entirely.
  • No price control, no import duty protection, no quality control order shields this industry. Equally, none blocks a new entrant.
  • Contingent liabilities and tax disputes for FY26 could not be quantified - the FY26 annual report was not publicly available as at 28-Aug-2026, with the 20th AGM scheduled for 28-Sep-2026. This is an explicit data gap, not a clean bill of health.

Geographic and geopolitical

Oman operations sit near, though outside, the Strait of Hormuz. Management stated shipments have been affected but "not that much that it's a matter of concern". The Oman exposure is equity-accounted and small in profit terms today, but the capital committed to it is real and the exploration block will not generate revenue before about 2030.

External Headwinds & Tailwinds

Earnings here are driven by three things in order: government and private exploration budgets, diesel, and the monsoon. Only the first is a story anyone tells.

Tailwinds

  • Coal India outsources the majority of its detailed drilling - 5.80 of 10.00 lakh metres targeted to contractors in FY24-25, near 60% for non-CIL blocks. Verified from the Ministry of Coal annual report.
  • Rs 5,925 cr coal exploration programme for FY2026-27 to FY2030-31 covering 55.50 lakh metres - about 2.8x the company's current annual metreage, per year.
  • Coal output past 1 billion tonnes, captive and commercial mines above 200 MT for the first time. Every new block needs proving.
  • CBM policy support - a 5.0 MMSCMD national target for 2027-28, 16 blocks offered across the 2025 and 2026 rounds, and the company's anchor client bidding for three of them.
  • 77% private order book insulates the company from precisely the government under-disbursement problem documented in Section 20.

Headwinds

  • Groundwater funding collapsed - Jal Jeevan Mission revised estimate Rs 17,000 cr against a Rs 67,000 cr budget, with no central releases to states in FY25-26. Atal Bhujal Yojana ends Mar-2026.
  • Government exploration money is announced, not spent - NMET holds Rs 4,449 cr unspent, the critical-minerals mission spent 22% of its FY26 budget.
  • Global exploration budgets are falling - USD 12.40 bn in 2025, a third consecutive nominal decline, grassroots share at a record low 21%.
  • Reporting mine count is shrinking - 2,046 to 1,973 between FY24 and FY25.
  • Rig scarcity is self-correcting. The 24-27% margin is the signal that funds competitor fleets, and the company is already renting third-party rigs to meet demand.

Diesel and mobilisation. Diesel is the dominant consumable in a rig-hours business, and no fuel escalation clause is disclosed in any public document. A sustained crude move therefore lands in the margin rather than in the client's invoice. The company has never quantified the sensitivity and this note will not manufacture a figure - but on a 24% operating margin with fuel among the largest variable costs, the exposure is material and unhedged as far as public disclosure shows.

Monsoon. Q1 is structurally the weakest quarter and H2 structurally the strongest, because drilling stops when the ground floods and because clients push completion to the fiscal year end. Management notes the effect varies with where the fleet happens to be working - Q2 FY26 was strong because the rigs were in low-rainfall Rajasthan. This is genuine earnings volatility that no amount of order book removes.

Currency and geopolitics. Oman JV profits are earned in rial and translated, but nothing has been repatriated, so the practical currency exposure today is on an unremitted balance rather than on cash flow. Middle East shipping disruption has affected but not halted the Oman operations per management.

What The Multiple Is Being Paid On

The 17.1x is struck on trailing earnings whose margin, restated for the coal-trading exit, did not expand in FY26 - core operating margin went from about 24.6% to 24.0% (Section 8). The growth is genuine and volume-led; the margin is flat and, on management's own account, driven by a shortage of rigs. Price-to-earnings cross-checked against EV/EBITDA is the right primary frame. This is asset-heavy but it is not an asset play - the rigs have no scarcity value on a second-hand market and book value is not the story. Earnings drive it, so earnings multiples govern. A discounted cash flow is nonetheless mandatory here precisely because earnings and cash have diverged so sharply.

Reverse DCF first: what the price already assumes

Assumptions stated openly: enterprise value Rs 752 cr, TTM EBIT Rs 55 cr and NOPAT Rs 41.8 cr at a 24% tax rate, WACC 13.5% (cost of equity 14.5%, pre-tax cost of debt 9.5%, market-value weights), return on incremental capital 20% matching FY26 ROCE, terminal growth 5% with terminal ROIC fading to 15%.

  • No-growth value. Capitalising current NOPAT in perpetuity with zero reinvestment gives Rs 310 cr - just 41% of the Rs 752 cr enterprise value. Around 60% of what the market pays is growth not yet delivered.
  • Implied growth. To justify today's price the market needs 26.2% EBIT compounding for ten years, or 19.1% sustained for fifteen years.
  • The arithmetic that matters. Growth equals reinvestment rate multiplied by return on capital. At a 20% ROIC, growing at 26.2% demands a reinvestment rate of 131% of NOPAT. The company must put back more than it earns, every year, for a decade. Free cash flow stays negative through the entire explicit forecast and essentially all of the value sits in the terminal value.
This Is Not A Modelling Artefact

FY26 free cash flow was negative Rs 33 cr in the best profit year in the company's history, and cumulative three-year free cash flow is negative Rs 18.5 cr. The model is describing what already happens. At a 20% return on capital, self-funded growth caps out at 20% a year. The price demands 26%. The difference has to come from debt or from issuing shares - and the Rs 200 cr Phase-I coal-block commitment sits on top of that, against Rs 12 cr of operating cash flow. The current share count does not reflect the capital the current price requires.

The 52-week range of Rs 121 to Rs 288 is worth holding in mind: the market has recently paid both far less and far more than the base case, on a business whose fundamentals moved far less than the price did.

No replacement-cost or EV-per-rig valuation is offered. The rig fleet has no observable second-hand market in India and any per-rig figure would be invented. Sum-of-the-parts is likewise avoided: the Jharkhand block and the Oman JVs have no independently verifiable value today, and the honest treatment is to leave them in the bull case rather than to capitalise them into a base-case target.

Pre-Mortem & Anti-Thesis

It is 2029. The stock is at Rs 90. Here is the post-mortem that gets written, argued as sincerely as the bull case deserves to be argued against.

The short-seller's case, in eight points

  1. The margin never actually expanded, and the cycle can still turn. Restated for the exit of a near-zero-margin coal-trading business, core operating margin went from about 24.6% in FY25 to 24.0% in FY26 - it fell. The reported jump from 18.9% to 24.0% is the arithmetic of removing a fifth of the revenue base, a mechanism that cannot repeat, and management never mentioned it on any of its three earnings calls. Management attributes today's rates to "a huge shortage of resources" and calls the business "quite cyclical" in its own words. Metreage collapsed 70% peak to trough between FY20 and FY22 and has only just regained the FY20 level. When rig supply catches up with demand - funded by exactly these margins - a 24% margin becomes an 18% margin. On Rs 300 cr of revenue that is Rs 18 cr of operating profit gone, more than half of FY26 net profit.
  2. The profit never became cash, and never will at this growth rate. FY26 earned Rs 30.8 cr and collected Rs 12.0 cr. Receivables alone absorbed Rs 44.6 cr - more than the entire year's profit - while the credit-loss charge went down. Free cash flow was negative Rs 35.6 cr in the best year in company history, and cumulative three-year free cash flow is negative. Cash on hand fell to Rs 0.89 cr. This is structural: at a 20% return on capital, growing at 26% requires reinvesting more than you earn, forever.
  3. The order book is not what it says it is. The Rs 761 cr headline is quoted including GST against revenue booked net of it - roughly Rs 645 cr of recognisable revenue.   4. Two customers are 69% of the order book and one is 35-40% of revenue. The Reliance CBM contract runs on an extension exercisable at Reliance's option. Hindustan Zinc's Rs 307 cr award was won on relationship, not on a contractual moat. A procurement review at either counterparty removes more revenue than this company earns in profit over several years. Top three customers were already 48% of revenue in FY25 - a figure disclosed in a note and never mentioned to investors.
  5. The coal block is a Rs 200 cr bet financed by a company that generates Rs 12 cr. Phase-I capex equals the entire net worth. The mining lease has never been confirmed as granted. Three clearance gates remain. The government takes 24.25% of revenue before shareholders see anything - a fact disclosed only on an earnings call, never in an annual report, and never reconciled with the annual report's own projection of Rs 700-800 cr of revenue at 40-42% EBITDA. First production has already slipped from FY2028 to FY2028-29, and the investor presentation still prints the wrong year.
  6. The moat is being rented. Utilisation is "more than 100%" because work is subcontracted and rigs are hired in. Employee cost fell from 20.6% to 14.7% of service revenue while other operating expense rose from 29.8% to 42.8%. In FY25, 48.9% of the subcontract line was purchased from related parties. Rs 19.31 cr of capitalised exploration spend with no accounting policy at all. A mandatory ratio note containing two demonstrable errors. Base rates

Ask what usually happens to an asset-heavy Indian contractor that trades at 17x trailing earnings at a cyclical margin peak, with negative free cash flow, one customer at 35-40% of revenue, and a single-asset diversification larger than its net worth. The honest historical answer is that the multiple compresses before the earnings do, because the market re-rates cyclicality faster than it re-rates growth. It is worth remembering that this very stock traded at Rs 121 within the last twelve months, roughly 11x - so a de-rating to the bear case is not a hypothetical; it is where the stock was two seasons ago on similar fundamentals.

The counter-base-rate is equally real and belongs here. Contractors that successfully move upstream into owning the resource - where the clearance actually lands and the funding does not destroy the equity - re-rate to a different multiple entirely, because they stop being priced on contract renewals. That is the bull case in one sentence, and it is why the distribution in Section 14 is so wide rather than merely uncertain.

Investment Pitch

Both cases are put as strongly as the evidence allows. This note carries no rating and no recommendation - the reader decides.

The 90-second pitch to buy

India's largest organised private exploratory driller, at 17x earnings, with the highest return on capital in its peer set and no listed pure-play competitor to arbitrage it against.

The demand is verified by primary government data, not by management: Coal India's own technical arm plans to outsource the majority of its detailed drilling - 5.80 of 10.00 lakh metres - and the Ministry of Coal has committed Rs 5,925 cr and 55.50 lakh metres over five years, roughly 2.8 times this company's entire annual metreage, every year. The order book is at an all-time high of Rs 761 cr against Rs 216 cr just two years ago, 77% of it private-sector, anchored by a Rs 307 cr four-year Hindustan Zinc contract and an extended Reliance CBM mandate. Utilisation is above 100% and the fleet is growing.

Crucially, the order book is private, so the documented failure of government exploration money to actually get spent - and it is a genuine failure - lands on the narrative rather than on the next two years of revenue. Incremental returns on the drilling capex have run between 17.5% and 36% depending on how much credit you give the cycle, and even the low end clears a 14-15% cost of equity. Promoter pay is modest and falling as a share of profit, there is no ESOP dilution, cash on the balance sheet is demonstrably real, and CRISIL moved the outlook to Positive before the reported upgrade to BBB+.

On top of that sits free optionality the market is not paying for: a 266-hectare Jharkhand coal block with 84 mt of geological reserves where exploration is already complete, and two Oman joint ventures including a USD 125 mn ten-year services contract. If the block reaches production on schedule, the company stops being a contractor priced on contract renewals and starts being a resource owner priced on reserves. That is a different multiple entirely.

The 90-second pitch to exit or avoid

You are paying 17x for a margin that did not actually expand, at a company that does not convert profit into cash, and being asked to fund a mine you did not underwrite.

FY26 was the best year in the company's history and it collected 39 paise of cash per rupee of profit - 25 paise after adjusting for deferred tax. Receivables absorbed more cash than the entire year's profit while the credit-loss charge fell. Free cash flow was negative Rs 35.6 cr, cash fell to Rs 0.89 cr, and net debt nearly tripled - in the same period management described the company as "getting towards being debt-free". The reverse DCF says the price requires 26% EBIT compounding for a decade, which at this return on capital demands reinvesting 131% of after-tax profit every year. That capital does not exist inside the business, and the share count does not yet reflect it.

The concentration is extreme: about 60% of revenue in two contracts, one client at 35-40%, on terms the client controls. The margin that drives the whole story is one management itself calls cyclical, on a metreage series that fell 70% peak to trough within the last six years, and it is increasingly delivered on subcontracted and hired capacity rather than the owned fleet that is supposed to be the moat.

Then the governance texture: promoters put Rs 30.0 cr into self-allotted warrants at Rs 132.20 and took Rs 44.7 cr out selling at Rs 229-249, most recently on 18-Aug-2026, netting Rs 14.7 cr while paying no dividend for two years. A Rs 5 lakh audit fee, an internal auditor drawn from the accounts department three years running, all joint-venture profit unaudited, Rs 19.31 cr of capitalised exploration with no accounting policy, and a going-concern subsidiary carried at par. No institution owns this - zero DII across every quarter - while the retail shareholder count jumped 46% in the quarter the promoters sold.

For an existing holder the exit discipline is specific rather than atmospheric: sell if operating margin prints below 21% for two quarters, if debtor days pass 200, or if an equity raise is announced at a discount to fund the coal block.

Time horizon

The bull case needs three years, not one. The Hindustan Zinc contract runs about four years and only began ramping in Q1 FY27; the coal block cannot produce before FY2028-29 even on management's own corrected timeline; the Oman exploration block is a 2030 story. Anyone underwriting the upstream transition is underwriting a FY29 event and should expect two more years of negative free cash flow and probable dilution before it arrives. The bear case, by contrast, can resolve in two quarters - a single margin print below 21%, or one soft collection quarter, would do it. That asymmetry in timing, not just in price, is the thing to hold onto.

"This is a bet on India's largest private driller converting a record Rs 761 crore order book and a 266-hectare coal block into owned reserves - priced as if today's peak-cycle margin is permanent and the Rs 200 crore of capital to get there is already in hand - and it breaks if the margin mean-reverts before the mine is funded."

The Funding Gap, With Numbers

Total rated bank facilities are Rs 83.69 cr and have not been enhanced since October 2024 - Rs 41.69 cr of bank guarantees and Rs 42.00 cr of cash credit, with fund-based utilisation averaging 77%. The performance guarantee required for the coal block alone is Rs 70-80 cr, more than the entire existing guarantee limit, which is already committed to live contracts. Against a Rs 200 cr Phase-I requirement sit FY26 operating cash flow of Rs 12 cr, negative free cash flow of Rs 33 cr, and about Rs 28 cr of warrant proceeds that CRISIL says are earmarked for rig additions and general corporate purposes, with FY27 rig capex of Rs 40-50 cr already planned. CRISIL flags coal-block funding as "a key monitorable". The Rs 200 cr is not funded from any identified source, and an equity raise is the most likely resolution - which is precisely the question management declined to answer when asked about future dilution.

Growth strategy in management's own framing

The Chairman describes three businesses: exploration services, Oman, and coal mining.

  1. Exploration services. Nineteen years, 165 projects, 3.3 million metres, ~30% of India's hydraulic exploration rig fleet, zero lost-time injuries. This is the entire current profit and the only strand with a track record. Capability proven; the constraint is working capital, not competence. Belongs in the forecast.
  2. Oman. The drilling and mining-services arm extends demonstrated capability and is running. The 17.5% exploration-block interest requires up to nine years of exploration before exploitation and no cash has ever been repatriated from either. Services arm extends capability; the equity arm is a capital call. Option bucket.
  3. Owning and operating a coal mine. A fundamentally different business - mine planning, statutory clearances, overburden removal, coal logistics and marketing, none of which the company has done. No mine manager, mining engineer or statutory mine official has been disclosed as hired. Management's own answer when pushed is telling: "we shall be in a position to appoint an MDO in future if we desire" - the plan is to subcontract the very capability the strand requires. Requires capability not hired for, funded by capital not raised, on a timeline that has already slipped two years. Option bucket, and the largest capital call in company history.

The Joint MD's defence deserves airing: "Our family is from mining background... it is not unfamiliar terrain for us." That is fair and worth some weight. It is also not an organisational capability, and it does not appear on the payroll or the balance sheet.

The Contradiction: Announced Versus Disbursed

Three independent official data points say the same thing. NMET, the exploration trust funded by a levy on mining royalties, had collected Rs 5,349.91 cr over its life and spent only Rs 900.87 cr - leaving Rs 4,449.04 cr, or 78.5%, unspent. The National Critical Mineral Mission was budgeted Rs 410 cr for FY2025-26 and its revised estimate came in at Rs 90 cr - 22% of budget. And Jal Jeevan Mission, which funds the groundwater vertical, was budgeted Rs 67,000 cr for FY2025-26 against a revised estimate of Rs 17,000 cr, with no central funds released to states at all that year. Management describes an exploration boom in which "all the domains are firing well". On the government side of the ledger, the money is being announced, not disbursed.

Where the contradiction does and does not bite

This is the part that a one-sided reading gets wrong in either direction, so it is worth being precise.

  • It bites hardest on groundwater. The aquifer-mapping and borewell vertical is funded almost entirely by Jal Jeevan Mission and Atal Bhujal Yojana. JJM's disbursement collapsed 75% in FY25-26 and Atal Bhujal's term ends in March 2026 with an FY26-27 allocation of Rs 0.13 cr. The company won an Rs 18.71 cr Odisha borewell order on 13-Aug-2026, so work is still flowing at state level - but the central funding line behind this vertical contracted sharply in the year just ended, and FY26-27 releases of about Rs 6,155 cr against a Rs 67,670 cr budget are a fraction of plan this far into the year.
  • It bites on the critical-minerals story. The narrative that carries the highest multiple in this sector - critical minerals, rare earths, the Exploration Licence regime - is the one with the weakest disbursement record. The first EL auction produced preferred bidders for only 7 of 13 blocks, several critical-mineral auctions since 2023 have been cancelled for want of qualified bidders, and the mission spent 22% of its first full-year budget. Management named "rare earths, critical minerals" among its new opportunity areas. Treat that as option value with a long fuse, not as pipeline.
  • It barely bites on the company's actual revenue. And this is the crucial point. About 77% of the order book is private sector - Reliance, Hindustan Zinc, Vedanta, Hindalco - and the two largest contracts, Rs 307 cr and over Rs 166 cr, are both private. The company has, whether by design or luck, positioned itself where the money is genuinely being spent. The government under-disbursement is a real risk to the narrative and to the groundwater line; it is a much smaller risk to the next two years of revenue.
