Jindal Drilling & Industries

NSE: JINDRILL  ·  BSE: 511034  ·  CMP INR 603  ·  13-Jul-2026  ·  Data as of Q4/FY26 (Mar-2026)

India's largest private offshore drilling-rig contractor to ONGC, riding a genuine jack-up day-rate upcycle: FY26 revenue INR 997 Cr, EBITDA up to INR 349 Cr (35% margin), nearly debt-free - and optically cheap at ~8x earnings and below book. Top 3 Positives

  • A real offshore upcycle ridden from a fortress balance sheet. FY26 revenue INR 997 Cr (+20%), EBITDA INR 349 Cr at a 35% margin - the best in a decade - as ONGC day-rates re-set higher. Crucially, borrowings have fallen 294 -> 164 -> 93 Cr and the company is near-debt-free with an investment book. Leverage is exactly what bankrupted the last-cycle comparables (Aban Offshore, Shiv-Vani); Jindal has removed that failure mode.
  • Structural jack-up scarcity gives genuine re-pricing optionality. ~30% of the ~471-rig global jack-up fleet is over 35 years old and the orderbook is thin (~20 newbuilds vs ~120 at the 2013-14 peak). When Jindal Supreme re-contracted with ONGC it jumped from ~USD 40,700/day to ~USD 88,859/day - the operating leverage on a fixed cost base is enormous, and the order book is contracted through FY27.
  • Optically cheap, with the promoter buying. ~8x earnings, EV/EBITDA ~5x, and trading around book value (BV INR 628 vs CMP 603) against a 15% ROCE. The promoter lifted its stake from 64.4% to 66.8% - roughly +2 points in a single quarter (Sep->Dec 2025) - into share-price weakness, i.e. voting with its own wallet.

Top 3 Risks

  • A third to a half of pre-tax profit is non-operating - and it lies about the trend. Reported PBT swung from +INR 173 Cr (Sep-2025) to -INR 45 Cr (Dec-2025) on "other income" that went +121 to -77. The main driver: a ~INR 100 Cr ONGC arbitration award booked in Q2FY26 and then reversed in Q3FY26 after ONGC appealed to the Supreme Court. Strip the arbitration, the investment-book mark-to-market and the equity-method JV profit, and the true operating PBT is a much smaller, steadier ~INR 40-67 Cr a quarter. The ~8x P/E is on flattered earnings.
  • Day-rates are rolling over, and the single customer is fighting back. Global jack-up rates fell ~17% in 2025; Jindal Explorer re-contracted DOWN to ~USD 35,139/day. In Apr-2026 ONGC cancelled a four-rig tender citing a "~60% escalation" and possible "collusive practices", is negotiating rates lower, and plans to build its OWN jack-ups (~USD 500-550 mn) - a structural threat to the third-party charter model on which ~90%+ of revenue depends.
  • No institutional anchor, and related-party plumbing. DII and mutual-fund ownership is literally zero, no marquee investor holds it, retail holders went 18,000 -> 41,600 in two years, and every large "bulk deal" is a proprietary/HFT desk churning the tape. One of the rigs (Jindal Explorer) is owned by sister-company Maharashtra Seamless and merely "facilitated through" Jindal Drilling; a meaningful slice of profit sits in offshore JVs outside the listed operating entity.

"This is a bet on India's largest private offshore driller compounding a genuine jack-up upcycle from a near-debt-free balance sheet - priced at ~8x and around book as if the cycle has already peaked - and it breaks if ONGC re-prices the fleet down as day-rates normalise while the non-operating income that flatters the P/E quietly reverses."

SECTION 01

Industry & Sector Analysis

Jindal Drilling sells one thing to essentially one buyer: offshore jack-up rig-days to ONGC in India's shallow waters. Its fortunes are therefore a levered play on the global jack-up day-rate cycle - which, after a powerful 2021-2024 recovery, is now plateauing to rolling over, even as an ageing world fleet keeps a structural floor under rates.

What the industry is. Offshore contract drilling supplies mobile rigs (and crews) that drill and work over oil and gas wells at sea, on multi-year day-rate charters. The relevant sub-segment for Jindal is the jack-up - a self-elevating shallow-water rig for water depths up to ~350-400 ft - as distinct from the deepwater drillships and semi-submersibles that ONGC tenders separately. Demand is derived from upstream E&P capex, which in turn tracks oil prices with a lag; supply is a slow-moving global fleet of ~470 marketed jack-ups whose economics turn on utilisation and day-rate.

Cycle position: past the inflection, now normalising. The 2021-2024 upcycle was real and large - Middle East benchmark jack-up rates ran ~USD 108,000/day (2023), the tightest market in a decade. But the cycle has turned: average Middle East rates eased to ~USD 92,000 (2024) and ~USD 90,000 (2025), and 2026 forecasts sit in the low-to-high USD 80,000s. Global marketed jack-up rates fell roughly 17% through 2025. The proximate cause is a supply shock: Saudi Aramco suspended ~36 jack-ups from Apr-2024 (capping production at 12 mbpd), and roughly 40% of those released units migrated to the Far East / South-East Asia - precisely the "regular" (non-premium) tier that competes closest to Indian ONGC work. Utilisation remains healthy (~89-90%) and Aramco is now recalling a handful of rigs from early 2026, but the tape is normalising down, not up. This is the single most important fact for the name: a low P/E on a driller as day-rates roll over is the textbook definition of a potential value trap, not automatically a bargain.

Cycle read

The last time global jack-up rates peaked (2013-14) the Indian offshore drillers that had geared into the top - Aban Offshore, Shiv-Vani - did not survive the down-leg. The cycle risk is not hypothetical; it is the base rate for this sub-sector (see Section 15).

The structural floor: an old fleet nobody is replacing. Against the cyclical softening sits a genuine multi-year support. Roughly 30% of the ~471-rig global jack-up fleet is over 35 years old, and the newbuild orderbook has collapsed to ~20 units versus ~120 at the 2013-14 peak - shipyards, financiers and buyers have all been burned. Aging attrition plus a thin pipeline means that if E&P demand merely holds, effective supply tightens over the back half of the decade. The bull case for day-rates is scarcity; the bear case is that ONGC's shallow-water programme is not big enough to absorb the Aramco-released tonnage at the old rates.

India / ONGC demand - the whole market for this company. ONGC hires ~30+ jack-up rigs from third parties for its Western Offshore (Mumbai High) and Eastern Offshore fields; Jindal is the largest private Indian owner-operator in that pool. ONGC's exploration capex is guided up from ~INR 10,000 Cr (FY26) toward ~INR 12,000 Cr (FY27, +20%), funding 100+ wells - a real domestic volume tailwind. Two caveats temper it: (1) ONGC's much-touted USD 18-20 bn tender is for deepwater drillships/semis, outside Jindal's jack-up fleet; and (2) in Apr-2026 ONGC cancelled a four-rig jack-up tender, alleging a "~60% escalation" (day-rate quotes jumping ~USD 35,600 to ~USD 56,200 in nine months) and possible "collusive practices" - a clear signal that the monopsony buyer is actively pushing back on rate inflation (Section 9).

Regulatory architecture. Upstream E&P is administered by the DGH under the Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas (established 1993; oversees NELP/HELP/OALP/DSF). ONGC procures rigs through open International Competitive Bidding on a two-bid, e-procurement basis, historically on a three-year firm charter (moving toward five years on some recent tenders). Since 01-Apr-2022 ONGC/MoPNG PSUs follow the DPIIT "Public Procurement (Preference to Make in India)" order - a Class-I local supplier (≥50% local content) can earn a purchase preference - which structurally favours a domestic operator like Jindal over pure foreign charterers, though the rig-specific local-content threshold under the current regime is not publicly pinned down.

Who captures value. The chain runs rig-owner/charterer (Jindal) -> national oil company (ONGC, the gatekeeper) -> the barrels. The driller earns a day-rate spread over a largely fixed operating cost base, so incremental day-rate drops almost entirely to EBIT - superb operating leverage on the way up, brutal on the way down. The single customer, ONGC, holds the pricing whip through its tender design; the driller's leverage is scarcity of suitable rigs and an incumbent operating record.

SECTION 02

How Jindal Drilling Actually Makes Money

Briefing note - the company, end to end

Boss, in plain terms: Jindal Drilling is a rig landlord to ONGC. It owns (or bare-boat charters) offshore jack-up drilling rigs, mans and maintains them, and hires them out to ONGC on multi-year day-rate contracts to drill wells in the shallow waters off Mumbai. It earns a fixed USD day-rate per rig-day, whether ONGC strikes oil or not - so it is a service contractor, not an explorer, and carries no reserves risk. Alongside the rigs it runs a smaller oilfield-services arm (directional drilling, measurement-while-drilling, mud-logging) that sells technical well services, historically via foreign-technology tie-ups.

The important subtlety is the corporate plumbing. The rig-charter revenue that bills ONGC books almost entirely in the listed standalone entity (FY26 sales INR 997 Cr, PAT INR 173 Cr). But a chunk of the rig-owning economics has historically sat in offshore joint ventures (Singapore-based Discovery Drilling Pte and associates), which are equity-accounted - their profit shows up as a single "share of profit of JV" line that lifted consolidated PAT to INR 216 Cr in FY25 (a ~INR 75 Cr JV contribution) and ~INR 211 Cr in FY26. In Mar-2025 the company bought the rig Jindal Pioneer out of that JV for ~USD 75 mn, which is why fixed assets jumped from INR 551 Cr to INR 1,294 Cr and the "investments" line fell. Unknown unknowns - what a first read misses

  • Single-customer, single-basin. ONGC is effectively the only customer and Indian Western Offshore effectively the only market. A tender cancellation, a rate reset or a payment delay from ONGC hits the whole P&L at once - there is no second leg.

  • USD on both sides. Revenues and much of the cost base are dollar-denominated - a partial natural hedge, so a weak rupee helps translated revenue but less than a naive model would assume.
SECTION 03

Competitive Landscape & Peer Benchmarking

Jindal's competitive position is defined by a graveyard. The two largest Indian offshore/onshore drillers of the last cycle - Aban Offshore and Shiv-Vani - were destroyed by leverage, leaving Jindal as the last major domestic private jack-up owner standing. Its real competition for ONGC tenders is now global charterers, and its edge over them is a clean balance sheet and domestic preference, not technology or scale.

The real competition: global charterers

For any ONGC jack-up tender Jindal actually bids against international rig owners - Shelf Drilling, Borr Drilling, ADES, COSL (China), Valaris and others - who have far larger fleets and can redeploy rigs released from Saudi Aramco into the Indian market. This is precisely why ONGC's Apr-2026 tender saw "60% escalation" and why ONGC pushed back: the global players set the marginal price. Jindal's counter-advantages are (1) domestic Class-I local-supplier preference, (2) an established ONGC operating track record and relationships, and (3) rigs already mobilised in-country (no mobilisation cost/time). These are real but modest edges on price, not a moat that lets it dictate terms.

Where Jindal ranks

Scale: small globally (~5 rigs) but the largest Indian private offshore driller. Balance sheet: best-in-class in the domestic peer set (near-debt-free) - the dimension that actually matters in this industry. Distress-driven share gain: the exit of Aban and Shiv-Vani has already happened; Jindal is the beneficiary, which is part of why it survived to ride this upcycle. The gap-to-leader (global charterers) on fleet size and technology is wide and not closing - Jindal competes on survivability and locality, not leadership.

SECTION 04

Competitive Advantage: 6 Cs Moat

Be honest: this is a narrow, survivability-based moat, not a pricing moat. The one genuinely strong "C" is Capital - the balance sheet. Everything else is moderate-to-weak, because the customer, not the company, holds the pricing power.

CostModerate

Some structural edge as a domestic operator - rigs already in-country (no mobilisation cost), lower overhead than global majors - but day-rates are set by competitive ONGC tender, so no durable cost-driven pricing advantage.

CapacityWeak

Owns ~5 scarce jack-ups, and global supply is tight - but capacity is just capital plus a rig order; any well-funded charterer can replicate it. Scarcity is an industry condition, not a Jindal-specific asset.

CapitalStrong

The real moat. Near-debt-free (borrowings INR 93 Cr) with an investment book, versus a sector whose leaders (Aban, Shiv-Vani) died of leverage. In a downcycle, survival IS the advantage - and Jindal can buy assets/contracts when others are forced sellers.

ChannelModerate

A decades-long ONGC operating relationship and Class-I domestic-preference access. But ONGC is a gatekeeper Jindal sells THROUGH, not a channel it owns - and every contract is competitively re-tendered.

Customer captivityWeak

Inverted: Jindal is captive to ONGC, not the reverse. Single customer, 3-5 year re-tenders, no switching cost, no brand pull. ONGC can cancel a tender (Apr-2026) or in-source rigs at will.

ComplianceModerate

ONGC pre-qualification, class certifications, HSE track record and local-content rules do raise entry barriers for a new domestic entrant - a modest, real barrier, but one every incumbent charterer clears.

Most important moat source and its half-life. The load-bearing advantage is Capital - the fortress balance sheet - reinforced by ONGC incumbency. Together they make Jindal a survivor that can compound through a cycle where leveraged peers cannot. But this is a moat against ruin, not a moat around pricing: it does not stop day-rates falling, it just ensures Jindal is still standing (and possibly buying) when they do. Its half-life is roughly one full cycle - it is only as durable as management's discipline in not re-leveraging at the top, which the FY20 impairment shows is the exact mistake this sector makes.

Revenue and margin. The operating story is legitimate. Revenue compounded ~24% over three years and EBITDA margin expanded from 23% (FY24) to 35% (FY26) as older rigs re-contracted at materially higher day-rates - operating leverage on a fixed cost base. This is real and it is the bull case. It is also, by construction, cyclical: the same leverage that took margin to 35% will take it back down if ONGC re-prices the fleet lower (Explorer at USD 35k is the warning shot). Depreciation nearly doubling (INR 64 Cr -> 151 Cr) reflects the Jindal Pioneer acquisition and rig capitalisation - a real cash cost of running an owned fleet that will keep rising.

The consolidated-vs-standalone bridge is itself a signal. Note the two net-profit rows. In FY23-FY24 consolidated PAT was below standalone (by ~15 and ~63 Cr) - the offshore JVs were a drag. In FY25-FY26 they flipped to a ~INR 75 Cr and ~INR 38 Cr contribution. That ~INR 140 Cr swing is the offshore-JV rigs moving from idle/low-rate to re-contracted - a second, less visible layer of the same day-rate cycle, carried through an equity-method line rather than revenue. It cuts both ways: JV profitability can fall as fast as it rose.

Return ratios. Reported ROCE is ~15-16% and ROE ~12.5% (FY26). But because ~30-50% of PBT is non-operating, the operating return on the rig capital employed is lower than the headline - a large, low-yielding investment book sits inside the capital base and inflates neither its own return nor the denominator's efficiency. Incremental ROCE on the FY24-25 rig capex (roughly INR 700+ Cr into fixed assets) is the number that matters, and it looks healthy at current day-rates - the entire question is whether current day-rates hold.

Working capital. Debtor days have actually improved: 112 (FY24) -> 90 (FY25) -> 82 (FY26). ONGC is a slow but ultimately certain payer, and there is no channel-stuffing dynamic here (one customer, contracted day-rates). Working capital is not the risk in this business - the day-rate is.

Cash flow. Cash conversion across the cycle is decent but lumpy. Reported operating cash flow (consolidated) was INR 64 Cr (FY22), -44 Cr (FY23), a very large 864 Cr (FY24), and 178 Cr (FY25). The FY24 figure looks anomalous relative to ~INR 89 Cr of PBT that year and is almost certainly distorted by working-capital and JV/rig-transaction mechanics - treat it with caution rather than as sustainable free cash. FY26 consolidated cash flow is not yet published (the FY26 annual report is not out as of Jul-2026). Aggregating FY22-FY25, operating cash comfortably exceeds cumulative PAT, so - unlike a working-capital-trap business - the cash does broadly follow the profit; the caveat is that the reported "profit" itself is inflated by non-cash mark-to-market other income.

Balance sheet - the genuine differentiator. Borrowings have fallen from INR 294 Cr (FY24) to 164 Cr (FY25) to 93 Cr (FY26) against reserves of ~INR 1,806 Cr; the company is effectively net-cash once the investment book (~INR 220 Cr consolidated) is netted. This is the whole reason the Aban/Shiv-Vani base rate need not repeat: those firms died of leverage into a downcycle; Jindal enters any downcycle with almost none. Off-balance-sheet items (rig-import customs/service-tax disputes) sit in contingent liabilities and are covered in Section 6/9.

SECTION 06

Earnings Quality & Forensic Checks

The books are clean and the cash is broadly real - this is not an accounting-fraud story. The earnings-quality problem is different and more subtle: the reported profit is a volatile blend of operating rig earnings, equity-method JV profit, mark-to-market on a financial portfolio, and one-off legal items - so the headline number and its ~8x multiple mislead on both level and trend.

Earnings quality: 6 / 10 - clean audit, real cash generation and a strong balance sheet, marked down for heavy non-operating income, mark-to-market volatility, a booked-then-reversed legal gain, related-party fleet economics, and profit that sits partly outside the listed entity.

Other-income dependence - the headline check

Other income was 50% of PBT in FY25 and 30% in FY26, and it is not stable "treasury yield" income - it is lumpy. The FY26 quarterly path (18, 121, -77, 18) decomposes into roughly USD-neutral base income of ~INR 18-23 Cr/quarter plus a ~INR 100 Cr ONGC arbitration award booked in Q2FY26 and reversed in Q3FY26 when ONGC took the matter to the Supreme Court and the auditors advised de-recognition. On top of that, a quoted investment portfolio produces fair-value (mark-to-market) gains and losses under Ind AS 109 that flow through the P&L. The precise FY26 split of arbitration vs mark-to-market vs base income cannot be pinned from public quarterly notes alone and needs the FY26 annual report (not yet published) - flagged.

Red flag - profit composition

Two distinct non-operating layers inflate the multiple: (1) a booked-then-reversed ~INR 100 Cr legal award still sub-judice at the Supreme Court - a binary item that could swing future quarters either way; and (2) mark-to-market on a financial book that has no connection to drilling. Neither is a repeatable operating earning. On operating PBT (ex-OI) of ~INR 190 Cr, the true operating P/E is closer to ~11-13x than the headline ~8x.

Related-party transactions - the fleet is partly intra-group

The clearest governance-flavoured earnings-quality flag is that Jindal Explorer, one of the ONGC rigs, is owned by sister-company Maharashtra Seamless Ltd and only "facilitated through" Jindal Drilling. Group-owned rigs routed through the listco, and an investment book that has historically included group/associate holdings, mean a slice of both revenue attribution and portfolio value is related-party in nature. This is not inherently abusive - it may be efficient use of group balance sheets - but it means an outside shareholder cannot cleanly separate Jindal Drilling's own economics from the D.P. Jindal group's. The exact quantum and pricing of related-party transactions needs the annual-report RPT schedule (Section 7).

The rest of the forensic battery

  • CWIP aging: negligible (INR 7 Cr, FY26) - no capital parked indefinitely in work-in-progress. Pass
  • Effective tax rate: volatile - 42% (FY24), 18% (FY25), 22% (FY26). The swings track the non-operating items (arbitration, JV profit, MTM) which carry different tax treatment; not a cash-tax-avoidance flag, but it makes normalised earnings harder to model. Watch
  • Auditor: M/s Kanodia Sanyal & Associates. The FY26 audited results are clean (no qualification surfaced). It is a mid-tier firm rather than a Big-4/large-national name - not a red flag in itself, but worth noting for a company with offshore JVs and a financial portfolio of this complexity. Tenure and any prior change should be checked against the annual report. Watch
  • Contingent liabilities: offshore drillers characteristically carry sizeable customs / service-tax / GST disputes on rig imports and charter-hire, plus the live ONGC arbitration. The exact quantum as a % of net worth requires the annual-report schedule - flagged as an open item (Section 9). Open
  • Cash yield on investments: the investment book is large but low-cash-yielding (much of its return comes through mark-to-market rather than dividends/interest), which is why it flatters book value more than it flatters cash earnings. Watch
Base-rate memory - the FY20 write-down

Do not forget FY20: the company reported a catastrophic net loss of ~INR 591 Cr (OPM -267%) - a large rig impairment / write-down as the previous cycle rolled over. That is the same asset class, the same customer and the same management now reporting 35% margins. The FY20 scar is the most important single piece of evidence that this is a cyclical, impairment-prone business, not a compounder - and it is why the balance-sheet de-leveraging since then is the thesis' load-bearing wall.

Verdict. Earnings quality 6/10. The positives are genuine: clean audit, cash that broadly follows profit, falling receivable days, near-zero debt. The marks against are that the reported number is a poor proxy for repeatable operating earnings - inflated by non-operating income, carried partly in JVs and related-party rigs, and scarred by a prior cycle's impairment. An analyst who values this on reported EPS will overpay; one who values it on operating EBITDA and a normalised day-rate will not.

SECTION 07

Promoter, Governance & Capital Allocation

Jindal Drilling belongs to the D.P. Jindal group - a real, cash-generative industrial house, and NOT one of the larger, better-known Jindal groups. The capital-allocation record is a study in conservatism verging on hoarding: no dilution in a decade, minimal dividends, and a large financial investment book held alongside the operating business.

Which Jindal group this is

This is the D.P. Jindal (Dharam Pal Jindal) group, whose flagship is Maharashtra Seamless Ltd (seamless steel pipes/tubes), alongside Jindal Pipes, Jindal Drilling and related entities. It must not be confused with the far larger O.P. Jindal lineage - Sajjan Jindal's JSW, Naveen Jindal's Jindal Steel & Power, and Prithviraj Jindal's Jindal SAW - which are separate families and businesses. The D.P. Jindal group is smaller, older-economy and closely held. The single most important intra-group fact for this analysis is that Maharashtra Seamless owns the rig Jindal Explorer, operated for ONGC through Jindal Drilling - a live related-party link between the two listed companies (Sections 6, 10).

Ownership and alignment

  • Promoter holding 66.8%, raised ~+2 points into weakness in late 2025 (a genuine alignment signal). The largest promoter vehicles are Jindal Global Finance & Investment (~17.4%) and Babul Holdings (~3.45%); named family members on the register include Raghav Jindal (~0.67%) and Dharam Pal Jindal (~0.63%).
  • No equity dilution in a decade: share capital has been flat at ~INR 14 Cr throughout - no QIPs, no serial preferential allotments, no visible ESOP dilution. For minority holders this is a real positive; the per-share count is not being quietly inflated.

Capital allocation - conservative to a fault

Over the last decade the company earned well (FY15-17), took a brutal impairment (FY18-20), then recovered strongly (FY22-26). What has it done with the cash?

  • Deleveraged aggressively - borrowings down to INR 93 Cr. Unambiguously good given the sector.
  • Reinvested in rigs - the FY24-25 capex and the ~USD 75 mn Jindal Pioneer buy-in from the Singapore JV. Sensible if day-rates hold; the returns are cycle-dependent.
  • Held a large financial investment book rather than returning cash - the portfolio (part quoted, historically including group/associate exposure) generates the lumpy mark-to-market other income that clouds the P&L. This is the crux governance question: is surplus capital being productively deployed, or parked?
  • Paid trivial dividends - payout of just 1-3% (FY26: ~INR 1/share, ~0.17% yield) despite ~INR 1,800 Cr of reserves and near-zero debt.
Governance watch - hoarding vs allocation

A near-debt-free company earning 15% ROCE that pays a token dividend and holds a big low-cash-yield investment portfolio is choosing balance-sheet accumulation over shareholder return. That conservatism is a genuine strength in a cyclical, impairment-prone industry - but it also means minority holders get the cyclical risk while the surplus capital compounds inside a closely-held group vehicle. The related-party rig and offshore-JV structure sharpen the question of whose interest the capital ultimately serves.

Open items (not completed in this research pass, flagged): the detailed board/KMP roster and independence, any CFO/CS/auditor changes and reasons, promoter remuneration and any royalty/commission as % of PAT, the full related-party-transaction schedule (quantum and direction), and any promoter litigation or SEBI history. These should be read from the FY25 annual report and exchange disclosures before forming a final governance view.

SECTION 09

Regulatory & Company-Specific Risks

Jindal's regulatory life is dominated by one counterparty - ONGC - which is simultaneously its only real customer, its de-facto price regulator, and now its adversary in a Supreme Court arbitration. The classic sector disputes (rig-import customs, service tax) sit underneath.

The live matters

  • ONGC arbitration at the Supreme Court. A ~USD 14.7 mn (~INR 100 Cr) arbitral award in Jindal's favour was recognised in Q2FY26 and reversed in Q3FY26 after ONGC appealed to the Supreme Court and the auditors advised de-recognition pending the outcome. A ruling either way is a discrete, binary P&L event - upside if upheld, no further downside if struck (already reversed).
  • ONGC's Apr-2026 tender cancellation. ONGC cancelled a four-jack-up tender citing a "~60% escalation" (quotes rising ~USD 35,600 to ~USD 56,200/day in nine months) and "legitimate concerns regarding potential collusive practices". This is not aimed at Jindal specifically, but it is a loud signal that the monopsony buyer will not simply pay up as the cycle tightens - a direct cap on the re-contracting upside.
  • Rig-import customs / service-tax / GST disputes. Offshore drillers characteristically carry material contingent liabilities on customs duty and indirect tax on rig imports and charter-hire. The exact quantum and % of net worth for Jindal needs the annual-report contingent-liability schedule - flagged as an open item, not yet quantified in this note.

Procurement architecture (the rules of Jindal's only market)

  • ONGC hires rigs via open International Competitive Bidding, two-bid, e-procurement, on historically three-year (now sometimes five-year) firm charters, administered under the DGH / Ministry of Petroleum framework.
  • Since 01-Apr-2022, ONGC follows the DPIIT "Preference to Make in India" order: a Class-I local supplier (≥50% local content) can earn a purchase preference - a structural tailwind for a domestic operator versus pure foreign charterers, though the rig-specific local-content threshold under the current regime is not publicly confirmed.

Concentration & rating

Concentration risk - the defining company-specific risk

One customer (ONGC), one basin (Indian Western Offshore), one product (jack-up rig-days), one currency (USD). Any adverse ONGC action - a tender cancellation, a rate reset, a payment delay, or the strategic decision to build its own rigs - hits the entire P&L simultaneously with no offsetting leg. This is the highest-conviction risk in the whole file.

Credit rating. The company is rated by CRISIL, with the latest rationale dated 15-May-2026 (prior actions 24-Sep-2025, 01-Jul-2024, 22-Aug-2023). The near-debt-free balance sheet and improved cash generation are supportive; the specific grade, outlook and the risks CRISIL flags in its rationale were not captured in this note's research pass and should be read directly from the CRISIL document (link in Sources) before relying on the rating - flagged as an open item.

Regulatory tailwinds

Not everything is a threat. ONGC's rising exploration capex (toward ~INR 12,000 Cr in FY27), the domestic-preference procurement regime, an energy-security mandate that keeps ONGC drilling through oil-price dips, and an ageing global rig fleet all support demand for an incumbent domestic operator. The tension in the whole thesis is that these tailwinds are real but the same buyer that provides them also controls the price.

SECTION 11

External Headwinds & Tailwinds

The counter-intuitive point: Jindal has almost no direct crude sensitivity in the near term. Its rates are fixed in USD and contracted through FY27, so the real macro drivers are the re-contracting day-rate (a lagged function of crude and global supply) and the rupee.

The macro drivers, honestly framed

  • Crude (Brent) - indirect and lagged, not direct. There is no crude line in the P&L. Crude bites only at two points: when contracts roll (re-contracting day-rates) and through ONGC's tender volume. ONGC's programme is relatively price-insensitive (national energy security), which dampens - but does not remove - the crude beta. Model a re-contracting/volume sensitivity, not a spot-crude one.
  • USD/INR - a genuine tailwind. At ~95.5 (Jul-2026) the rupee is materially weaker than the ~83-86 of FY24-25, lifting INR-translated USD revenue. But both revenues and much of the cost base are USD-denominated - a partial natural hedge - so the benefit is real but smaller than a naive translation implies.
  • Global jack-up cycle - the key headwind. Rates fell ~17% in 2025; Middle East averages went USD 108k (2023) -> 92k (2024) -> 90k (2025) -> low-USD-80ks (2026 forecast). Saudi Aramco's suspension of ~36 jack-ups from Apr-2024 pushed ~40% of released units into the Far East / SE Asia, pressuring exactly the tier that competes for ONGC work. Offsetting: ~90% utilisation, an ageing fleet, a thin newbuild book, and Aramco now recalling a few rigs from early 2026.

SECTION 15

Pre-Mortem & Anti-Thesis

Assume it is three years from now and the position has lost half its value. Here is the short-seller's post-mortem, argued sincerely.

The short case

  1. You bought peak earnings at peak margins. 35% EBITDA margin and INR 349 Cr EBITDA were the top of a day-rate cycle that had already begun rolling over (global rates -17% in 2025). Normalised margins are closer to 25%, and you paid ~5x EV on the peak, which is ~7x on the normal.
  2. The multiple was a mirage. The "8x P/E" was flattered by ~30-50% non-operating income - a booked-then-reversed arbitration award and mark-to-market on a financial book. The real operating P/E was ~12x for a single-customer cyclical - not cheap at all.
  3. The monopsony crushed you. ONGC cancelled tenders over "60% escalation", negotiated Star and Discovery-I down at re-contracting, and started building its own jack-ups. One customer with the pricing whip is a structural, not a temporary, ceiling.
  4. A rig got impaired again. As day-rates fell, an ageing rig's carrying value was written down - exactly as in FY20 (-INR 591 Cr). The asset base is impairment-prone the moment the cycle turns.
  5. The capital was never yours. A token dividend, a big investment book with related-party exposure, one rig owned by a sister company, and a slice of profit in offshore JVs - surplus cash compounded inside a closely-held group, not returned to minorities.
  6. No one caught you. Zero DII/MF ownership, no marquee holder, a register that is 60%+ retail with prop desks churning the float - when sentiment turned there was no institutional bid to cushion the fall, and recurring ASM flags warned of exactly that.

Thesis-killers - specific, observable

  • Jindal Star or Discovery-I re-contracts below ~USD 45,000/day (confirms the down-leg).
  • ONGC firms up its own-jack-up build programme or repeatedly cancels/renegotiates third-party tenders (structural in-sourcing).
  • Any rig impairment or write-down appears in results (the FY20 pattern repeats).
  • Other income turns persistently negative (MTM losses) and/or the Supreme Court strikes the arbitration for good.
  • Promoter stops buying or starts selling; or cash is deployed into group entities rather than the core / shareholders.

SECTION 16

Investment Pitch

No rating. Two sincere cases, and the one line that frames the bet.

The 90-second pitch to buy

India's largest private offshore driller, riding a real jack-up upcycle from a fortress balance sheet - the one thing the last cycle's casualties (Aban, Shiv-Vani) lacked. FY26 revenue INR 997 Cr, EBITDA INR 349 Cr at 35%, borrowings down to INR 93 Cr, and it trades at ~5x EV/EBITDA and around book, with net cash plus a INR 220 Cr investment portfolio cushioning the downside. The promoter is buying (+2 points into weakness), there is no dilution, and a structurally scarce global jack-up fleet gives genuine re-pricing optionality - Jindal Supreme already jumped from USD 40k to USD 89k. With Star and Discovery-I due to re-contract and a Supreme Court arbitration that could restore ~INR 100 Cr, you are paid to own a survivor that the market has priced as if the cycle is already over.

The 90-second pitch to exit / avoid

This is peak earnings at peak margins in a cycle that has turned - global day-rates fell 17% in 2025 and Jindal Explorer just re-contracted DOWN to USD 35k. The 8x P/E is a mirage: strip the ~INR 100 Cr booked-then-reversed arbitration and the mark-to-market on a financial book and the real operating multiple is ~12x for a company with one customer that is actively fighting rate escalation (Apr-2026 tender cancellation) and planning to build its own rigs. Profit sits partly in offshore JVs and on a related-party rig; capital is hoarded in investments rather than returned; and there is no institutional anchor - zero DII/MF, a retail base that doubled, and prop desks churning the float.
