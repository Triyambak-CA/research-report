What the eight quarters actually say

The trough was Q2 FY26, and it was severe. Revenue fell 28.7% year on year to ₹135.59 cr, EBITDA margin collapsed to 12.53%, profit fell 77.4% to ₹4.25 cr. The cause was disclosed on the call: a three-and-a-half week standoff with the largest customer over US tariffs. From that quarter the company has gone 135.6, 278.0, 306.1, 360.7 - a 2.66 times increase in three quarters. That is a genuine inflection and no amount of forensic scepticism changes it.

Read the 130.4% headline honestly. Q1 FY27's growth is measured against Q1 FY26's ₹156.58 cr, itself a weak quarter. Against the company's own best prior quarter the sequential step-up is 17.9%.

Three quarterly anomalies worth a question
  • Q2 FY26 had a near-record gross margin in its worst quarter. 51.2% gross margin alongside a 12.53% EBITDA margin. A reported gross margin that rises while the business falls apart is being propped by the change-in-inventory line, not by pricing. Management effectively conceded it, attributing earlier gross margin improvement to "increased work in progress and production for future quarters" and the margin dip to "inventory build-up". MTAR's reported gross margin is materially sensitive to inventory capitalisation and is not a clean read on pricing.
  • FY26's first half to second half ratio landed at exactly 2.00. H1 ₹292.18 cr, H2 ₹584.03 cr, against a guidance of "almost 2x". Revenue recognition that lands that precisely on a guided ratio warrants cut-off testing.
  • Q4 FY26 converted 71.6% of EBITDA to profit, against 41-59% in every other quarter. The implied gap between EBITDA and pre-tax profit is only about ₹2.4 cr, in a company running more than ₹6 cr of quarterly finance cost alone on lower debt a year earlier. This is unexplained by any accessible disclosure.

Earnings per share are still below where they were three years ago

FY23 delivered ₹33.62 of earnings per share. FY26 delivered ₹30.57 - 9.1% lower, three years later. FY24 profit fell 45.7% and FY25 fell a further 5.7%. Over the same three years the share price went from about ₹1,582 to ₹7,040. Every rupee of that move is multiple expansion, not earnings.

The margin break is equally clear. EBITDA margin was 26.85% in FY23. It has not been above 20% in a full year since. The structural break happened in FY24 and has been guided away every year since without being repaired.

Returns: the number depends on who is publishing it

Return on capital for FY26 has been published by this company at 12.1%, 12.39%, 17.2% and 18.1%, depending on the document. The audited annual report says 12.39%; the investor relations deck says 18.1%; the earnings call says 17.2%. For FY25 the range is 9.65% to 18.2%. Section 6 sets out why this matters. For the purpose of this section, the defensible figures are the independently reconcilable ones: return on equity of 8.66% in FY24, 7.53% in FY25 and 12.12% in FY26, each computed from a net-worth chain that reconciles to within ₹0.36 cr and matches the company's own stated returns for the two earlier years.

A business earning 7.5% on equity in FY25 and 12.1% in FY26 is earning at or below its cost of capital. That is the honest return picture underneath a 162x multiple.

MTAR carries between half and two thirds of a year's revenue as inventory

At the FY24 peak that was 445 days - roughly fifteen months of production cost sitting on the balance sheet. After three years of stated improvement, FY26 inventory of about ₹420 cr against ₹876 cr of revenue is still 335 days. At the company's 10.6% cost of debt that balance carries roughly ₹44 cr a year of pure carrying cost - equivalent to 47% of FY26 profit after tax.

This is the central economic fact about MTAR and it is why the reverse discounted cash flow in Section 14 produces the numbers it does.

Advances reverse the sign of the working capital ratio, but they are not where most of the cash came from

Customers paid MTAR ₹209.3 cr more in advance during FY26 than the year before. Two things follow, and they need separating because the obvious reading overstates one of them.

On the ratio, the effect is decisive. Net working capital computed from the balance sheet is ₹407.3 cr at FY25, 220 days of revenue, and ₹387.0 cr at FY26, 161 days. Hold the advance balance flat at its FY25 level and FY26 net working capital would be ₹596.3 cr, or 248 days - 28 days worse than FY25 rather than 59 days better. Every measure built on net working capital nets advances off, which is why they all improve while the cash conversion cycle, which never sees them, worsens from 369 days to 419.

On the cash, the effect is real but partial. Operating cash flow rose ₹95.65 cr year on year. Ranked, the working capital movements behind it were:

So the advances were the largest single line and were still mostly consumed by the inventory and receivables needed to execute the orders they were paid against. More than half the increase in operating cash flow is operating profit growth. Advance money remains a liability discharged in work rather than earnings, and it unwinds as the book is delivered. Management has not discussed it: the word "advance" appears nowhere in the Q1 FY27 earnings call transcript. Section 6, test four, reconciles all four published working capital measures.

The cumulative number

Over FY22 to FY26 MTAR reported ₹367 crore of accounting profit and ₹662 crore of EBITDA, and consumed ₹252 crore of cash. Free cash flow was negative in four of the five years and turned positive for the first time only in FY26, at ₹63 crore on the audited statements - and that first positive year was made entirely in its fourth quarter, on customer advances.

Through nine months of FY26 the business had consumed ₹22 cr of cash on ₹109 cr of EBITDA, a conversion of minus 20%, worse than any prior year. Two consecutive quarters of operating cash flow at roughly three times EBITDA, immediately after three quarters of negative-to-negligible cash generation, is not a working capital improvement. Section 6 sets out what it most probably is.

Balance sheet

Total assets grew 54.2% in FY26 against revenue growth of 29.6%, so asset turnover deteriorated. The current ratio fell from 2.15 to 1.56. Debt more than doubled to ₹376.57 cr and rose a further 12.5% to ₹423.6 cr by June. Against that sits ₹379 cr of investments, so the company describes itself as close to debt free at the net level - a claim examined in Section 6. Effective cost of debt is 10.60%, which is high for a borrower rated in the A band and points to a stack dominated by working-capital facilities. Borrowings are secured by current assets and property: the working capital is the collateral.

Off-balance-sheet items - bank guarantees, letters of credit, performance guarantees and lease commitments - are not disclosed in any accessible source for any year. For a company that exports the large majority of its output, imports raw material, and has just taken a ₹504 cr contract with a state nuclear operator, that is a meaningful gap.
