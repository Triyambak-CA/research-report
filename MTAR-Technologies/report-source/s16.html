Section 16

The investment pitch, both ways

No rating is given here, and none is implied. Both cases are argued as their strongest advocate would argue them, and the reader decides.

The ninety-second pitch to buy

MTAR makes the hot box - the core assembly - for the fuel cells that are becoming the fastest deployable answer to the single hardest constraint in technology right now, which is getting power to an AI data centre when the grid queue is five years long. Its customer just printed a billion-dollar quarter growing 165%, carries a USD 20 bn backlog, is doubling capacity, and has expanded one financing partnership from USD 5 bn to USD 25 bn in nine months. That demand is arriving as revenue, not as promises.

MTAR is not a speculative entrant to it. It has supplied this customer for fifteen years, its capacity plan is explicitly synchronised with the customer's, and the incremental capital required is remarkably small - roughly ₹50-60 cr buys 8,000 units of annual capacity against about ₹5.1 lakh of revenue per unit. That is an asset turn near seven times on marginal capital, which is why the ₹500 cr programme is only ten per cent of a ₹5,943 cr backlog.

Underneath it sits something rarer: a fifty-seven-year nuclear qualification in a supply chain where a new entrant needs seven to ten years to get accredited with no guarantee of an order. India has just repealed both its nuclear Acts and opened the sector to private capital. MTAR supplies the entire fuel handling system for a reactor and has just booked the largest nuclear order in its history.

And the operating inflection is already in the numbers. Quarterly revenue has gone ₹135.6 cr, ₹278.0 cr, ₹306.1 cr, ₹360.7 cr. EBITDA margin from 12.5% to 23.6%. Working capital from 229 days to under 100 on management's own target, with ₹247.69 cr of operating cash in a single quarter. Five years of no dilution whatsoever - the share count has not moved. Clean audit opinions. A credit rating upgraded in July. Seven analysts, all positive, one of them modelling a 66% revenue compound rate through FY29.

If the backlog converts at anything like the rate the last four quarters suggest, this is a company doing ₹2,300 cr of revenue in FY28 at a 23% margin, and ₹8,300 a share is 80 times that. You are buying the only listed Indian access point to the AI power build-out, with a nuclear option attached for free.

The ninety-second pitch to exit or avoid

You are paying 162 times trailing earnings, 97 times EBITDA and 26 times book - the most expensive of eight Indian peers on the two multiples that do not depend on an earnings base, after a 295% year - for a business whose earnings per share are still nine per cent below where they were three years ago and whose three-year revenue compound rate of 15.2% ranks sixth of eight.

The reverse discounted cash flow is unambiguous. On central assumptions the price requires a 55-60% revenue compound rate for ten years. At the most generous corner available - a margin never achieved, capital efficiency four times better than realised, and a cost of capital below any defensible estimate - it still requires 40%. MTAR's actual incremental sales-to-capital ratio is 0.61: every rupee of new sales has needed ₹1.6 of capital.

The record order book that justified the re-rating is one blanket purchase order from an undisclosed customer with no execution schedule at all, 44% of the book, and with one other unnamed order it is 94.9% of the record quarter. Concentration rose in the quarter management called diversified. Seventy per cent of forward capex goes into the same customer. That customer told its own shareholders it has multiple qualified suppliers and that no single supplier determines its destiny, and it does not name MTAR anywhere.

Over five years this company reported ₹367 cr of profit and consumed ₹252 cr of cash. The cash that finally arrived came in two quarters at roughly three times EBITDA, which is what customer advances look like, not earnings - and the FY26 balance sheet confirms it, with advances from customers up ₹209 cr in the year. A third of last year's growth in pre-tax profit was a currency gain. Management has hit 31% of its own quantifiable commitments, 10% on margin and 6% on capex dates, and stopped naming its largest customer on the last two calls while withdrawing the capacity roadmap. The promoters have halved their stake and were still selling five days ago, into the most severe exchange surveillance stage, in a stock where volume has fallen 96% and you may not be able to get out for days.

This exact setup - this company, this customer - already broke once in the last three years, and the stock fell 60% while revenue grew. For an existing holder the question is not whether the business is good. It is whether the next 5% of upside is worth the 70% of downside that a de-rating to its own five-year average multiple would produce.

The time horizon over which the bull case plays out

Eighteen to thirty months, and it is testable much sooner than that. The first genuine evidence arrives with the Q2 FY27 result in late October 2026: revenue of about ₹405 cr confirms the path, revenue at or below Q1's ₹360.7 cr with a flat order book says the largest order in the company's history has not started converting. The October capacity commissioning and the second-half start of nuclear execution both fall inside FY27. The full bull case - ₹2,300 cr of revenue at a 23.5% margin - is an FY28 outcome, so it resolves by roughly mid-2028.

The one-line summary

This is a bet on one American fuel-cell maker's AI data-centre order book converting into Indian hot boxes, priced as if MTAR compounds revenue at 55-60% a year for a decade, and it breaks if the blanket purchase order does not become firm call-offs on the schedule the market has assumed.

This report gives no buy, sell or hold recommendation and none should be inferred from it. It presents the evidence found, the evidence that could not be obtained, and both sides of the argument. The scenario prices are arithmetic on stated assumptions, not targets. Nothing here is investment advice, and the author holds no position in any security mentioned.
