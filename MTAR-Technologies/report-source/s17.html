Section 17

Capex deployment and return on capex

What this section cannot deliver, and why

The audited cash flow statements and the property, plant and equipment note for FY24, FY25 and FY26 could not be retrieved from this research environment. No capex table has been fabricated. Capital work in progress is not disclosed for any year, full-year depreciation is not disclosed for FY25 or FY26, and the split of capex between growth, maintenance and non-operating purposes is not disclosed by the company in any document. Those items are reported as missing rather than estimated, and the exact notes that would close them are named at the end of this section.

The FY26 investing outflow of ₹352.6 cr against capex of ₹128.3 cr leaves a gap of about ₹224 cr, which is the purchase of the short-term investment pile discussed in Section 6. That confirms the ₹238.69 cr of investments was bought with the fourth-quarter operating inflow plus new borrowing.

One aggregator figure is flagged as unusable and it matters

A data provider reported FY25 investing cash flow of about minus ₹100 cr described as an 86.9% improvement, which would imply FY24 investing outflow of roughly minus ₹763 cr against a balance sheet of about ₹1,000 cr. That is not credible as capital expenditure. It is almost certainly gross purchases of mutual funds and fixed deposits flowing through investing activities. MTAR's investing cash flow is contaminated by treasury churn and cannot be read as capex. Anyone modelling this company from an aggregator's investing line is modelling the wrong number.

The ₹500 cr programme is split roughly 70:30 in favour of clean energy. The capital allocation plan is a concentration increase, not a diversification. No analyst note reviewed framed it this way. MTAR is not choosing among independent reinvestment opportunities at a hurdle rate; it is matching one customer's capacity ladder, explicitly synchronised with that customer's own expansion.

The single large discretionary asset: unresolved, not absent

A deliberate search was made for a land purchase, head office, guest house or other non-operating asset buried inside a multi-year total. No evidence of one was found - but the two documents where one would appear, the property note and the related party note, could not be opened. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and a news search does not surface footnotes. The most likely location is the undisclosed land acquisition implied by the special economic zone relocation, which sits inside the ₹500 cr programme with no separate cost.

The replacement ratio

Strictly not computable, because maintenance capex is never split out. The only usable fragment: of the FY26 ₹100 cr plan, ₹70 cr was the oil and gas facility and therefore pure growth, leaving ₹30 cr for "sustenance and nuclear support" - and the nuclear support portion is itself growth. Against a derived FY26 depreciation of about ₹36 cr, that implies a replacement ratio of roughly 0.8 times or lower.

Read that with care. Spending less than depreciation on replacement is normal and defensible in a company with a young asset base in the middle of an expansion, and MTAR's machine base has roughly doubled. But a replacement ratio sustained below one across a revenue scale-up of 2.7 times would eventually surface as maintenance debt, and there is no disclosure that would let an investor see it coming.

The one computable increment. FY26 clean energy revenue of ₹615.4 cr was earned on capacity moving toward 12,000 units, implying roughly ₹5.1 lakh of revenue per unit of exit capacity. Applied to the 8,000 incremental units bought for ₹50-60 cr, that is about ₹410 cr of incremental revenue capacity for ₹50-60 cr of capital - an asset turn of roughly seven times, carrying perhaps ₹80 cr of incremental EBITDA at FY26 margins. That is an extremely high theoretical return on that specific increment, and it is the single best argument in the bull case. It is also a derivation on assumed full utilisation and a blended revenue per unit that includes non-fuel-cell products. It is not a company disclosure and must not be treated as one.

The hurdle it must clear cannot be stated, because MTAR's realised treasury yield is not disclosed anywhere - interest income is not separately reported in any accessible document. For a company that held ₹238.69 cr of investments at year end while paying 10.60% on its debt, that is a conspicuous gap.

Three reasons that number is probably too harsh, stated rather than buried
  1. Total assets is a poor denominator. FY26's 54% balance-sheet expansion was largely working capital and treasury investments, not fixed assets. The incremental fixed capital was far smaller.
  2. The numerator is truncated. The large FY26 capex - nuclear facility, hot box phases two and three, oil and gas - produces no operating profit until FY27. Nuclear execution starts in the second half of FY27. Charging the denominator without crediting the numerator understates the return.
  3. Q1 FY27 already invalidates the trailing picture. EBITDA margin of 23.54% against 19.5% for FY26, and ₹247.69 cr of operating cash flow in a single quarter.

The honest summary: on the only denominator that could be constructed, MTAR's FY24 to FY26 incremental return is about 7.5% pre-tax, well below its cost of equity. The denominator is contaminated and the numerator is pre-harvest. This question is genuinely unresolved on public evidence, and the FY26 cash flow statement and property note settle it.

Return on genuinely operating capital cannot be computed at all, because separating operating capital from the treasury pile requires balance-sheet detail that is not available.

Funding: debt, and a conspicuous absence

The ₹500 cr programme is to be funded by "a combination of internal accruals and debt". FY26 capex was guided as 70% debt funded. On 29-Jan-2026 the board approved raising borrowing limits to ₹800 cr for the company and ₹900 cr including subsidiaries, approved by postal ballot together with resolutions permitting the creation of charges on company assets. Gross debt has gone ₹177 cr, ₹377 cr, ₹424 cr across three balance-sheet dates. Finance cost in Q4 FY26 alone rose 62.7% year on year to ₹9.6 cr, before the programme is drawn.

The corporate action that is not in the guidance

MTAR has issued no equity in five years. The share count is fixed at 3,07,59,600. With the stock at ₹7,040 and a market value of ₹21,654 cr against trailing profit of ₹133 cr, an equity raise would be extraordinarily cheap capital - arguably the single most rational corporate action available to this management, and the obvious way to fund a ₹500 cr programme without pushing debt toward the ₹900 cr authority just taken. It is not mentioned in any guidance. Watch for it. It would also be, mechanically, the event most likely to break the price.

The capital allocation verdict

Yes, MTAR has a genuine reinvestment outlet, and it is unusually capital-light. ₹500 cr of capex against a ₹5,943 cr backlog is capital expenditure of roughly 10% of backlog. The hot box increment costs ₹50-60 cr for 8,000 units against roughly ₹5.1 lakh of revenue per unit. Management says nuclear needs almost no new capital. Working capital, which absorbed cash for four straight years, released ₹247.69 cr in a single quarter. On its own terms the reinvestment case is strong.

The caveat that governs the verdict

The reinvestment is not diversified and it is not chosen on a hurdle rate. Seventy per cent of it goes into matching one customer's capacity ladder, explicitly synchronised with that customer's own expansion. It is a high-return outlet only for as long as that customer's programme holds - and the FY24 experience, when guidance was cut by a third because clean energy shipments were deferred against confirmed orders, is the historical instance of that risk crystallising. The capital allocation decision being made here is a leveraged bet on one customer's capital expenditure cycle, funded increasingly with debt.

Should the company be distributing instead? On the arithmetic, no - a business with a genuine seven-times asset turn on its marginal capacity should reinvest, and it has paid no dividend since February 2022 for exactly that reason. But it should be funding that reinvestment with equity at 162 times earnings rather than with debt at 10.6%, and it is not.
