Section 15

Pre-mortem and anti-thesis

It is 2028. The stock is at ₹2,000. Here is the post-mortem someone will write, argued sincerely, from facts that are already on the record today.

The short seller's case, in eight points

  1. The record order book was never an order book. Forty-four per cent of it was a single blanket purchase agreement from an unnamed international customer, whose own exchange filing said the execution timeline "will be decided at a later stage". A blanket agreement is an umbrella against which a customer may call off quantities. Booking ₹2,278.96 cr of it as backlog, announcing a threefold order-book jump and letting the market read it as contracted revenue was an aggressive disclosure choice. When the call-offs came slower than the guidance implied, there was no contract to point to, because there was no schedule in the first place.
  2. The diversification never happened, and the company's own numbers said so at the time. In the very quarter management called the book diversified, clean energy went from 51.2% to 66.7% of it. Aerospace and defence fell from 14.0% to 7.4%. Nuclear was flat in absolute terms at ₹684 cr while producing ₹3.2 cr of quarterly revenue. Seventy per cent of the ₹500 cr capital programme went into the same customer. Every named diversifier failed: the battery storage customer was deleted from a slide without announcement, electrolysers were never commercialised, the oil and gas plant slipped four times and had its peak revenue guidance cut by more than 80%, and the data centre vertical was one ₹45 cr order whose products were never described.
  3. The cash was never there. Across FY22 to FY26 the company reported ₹367 cr of profit and consumed ₹252 cr of cash. Through nine months of FY26 operating cash flow was minus ₹22 cr on ₹109 cr of EBITDA. The entire year's cash arrived in one quarter at 354% of that quarter's EBITDA, and the next quarter repeated it at 291% and 69% of revenue. That was customer advances - a liability repayable in work - running through the operating line, and it reversed as the orders executed, taking the "net debt free" balance sheet and the "working capital days halved" headline with it.
  4. A third of the profit growth was a currency line, and it stopped. The FY26 accounts carry a ₹20.66 cr foreign exchange gain inside other income, up from ₹2.57 cr, whose ₹18.09 cr rise is 33.1% of the entire increase in pre-tax profit, and which is 89.5% of other income, on a rupee that fell 8.66%. ₹8.63 cr of it was unrealised. When the rupee stabilised, that line went back to a few crore and the reported growth rate halved. Gross margin had been falling the whole time anyway, from 49.4% to 45.61%, because the product mix was moving from precision machining to enclosures and cable harnesses.
  5. The guidance record told you everything. Twenty-six hits from eighty-three scored claims. One margin hit in ten attempts. Capex and commissioning dates hit six per cent of the time. In February 2025 management volunteered a ladder of 24%, 26% and 28% margins for FY26, FY27 and FY28; by May they had cut FY26 to 21% calling it a "basic minimum" the market could be "rest assured" about, and then missed it. The FY28 target was never withdrawn - it simply stopped being mentioned, and when an analyst asked about it directly the chief executive answered a different question.
  6. Disclosure went backwards exactly when it mattered. On the last two earnings calls before the peak, management never once said the customer's name. The hot box capacity roadmap, previously given in units with dates, was withdrawn and replaced with the word "multifold". Deflections quintupled. The company published its FY27 margin guidance three different ways on the same day, its return on capital at values from 9.65% to 18.2% for the same years, and its export share at 18% in the deck and 83% in the annual report. Meanwhile the compliance officer seat turned over three times in two years and the incumbent had four years of experience.
  7. The promoters were selling the whole way up. Holding halved from over 50% at listing to 29.36%, with a five-point drop in one quarter that was never explained. Eight sales by seven promoters against one buy, and that single buy was at one fifth of the selling price. Eighty-three per cent of the selling happened at and after the all-time high, while the stock was under the exchanges' most severe surveillance stage, the last sale five days before the report that called it out. The two smartest holders, a global private equity firm and its former India head, were already gone - the latter at ₹1,793, for a 47x return.
  8. It had happened once before, to this company, from this customer. In FY23 more than 70% of revenue came from the same customer. Order delays from a technology transition and rising US rates caused sharp revenue and profit declines and a 36% collapse in market value, while management kept guiding 30-35% growth. Three years later the same setup was priced at 162 times earnings, and almost nobody mentioned it.

Base rate: how these setups have historically resolved

The most useful base rate is MTAR's own

In September 2023 MTAR peaked at ₹2,920.35 on the same structure: a large order book, a dominant export customer, and a growth narrative. Nineteen months later it traded at ₹1,155.60, a drawdown of 60%. Over exactly that period revenue grew, from ₹574 cr to ₹676 cr. Earnings fell, because margin broke - and the multiple did the rest.

Growth was not the problem last time. The entry multiple was.

Two live examples sit in the same sector this year. Zen Technologies compounded revenue at 46.5% over three years, then saw FY26 revenue fall 29.4%; the stock is up 2.7% in a year. Walchandnagar Industries is a qualified nuclear supplier with the same policy tailwind MTAR is being valued on, and its three-year revenue compound rate is minus 5.1% with a loss in FY26. Being qualified is not the same as converting.

The broader pattern for Indian mid-cap engineering names that re-rate past 150 times on a single-customer export ramp is that they resolve by multiple compression rather than by earnings failure. The earnings often arrive. The stock halves anyway, because the entry multiple did the work. That is the single most likely way this ends badly, and it does not require anything in the bull case to be wrong.

What would make the bear wrong

Intellectual honesty requires stating this too. If the blanket purchase order converts on schedule, if the October capacity milestone lands, if nuclear starts billing in the second half as promised, and if the margin holds at 23-24% through a doubling of revenue, then FY28 earnings per share of ₹104 is achievable and 80 times that is ₹8,300. The company would then be earning ₹320 cr of profit on capital it has largely already deployed, with a reinvestment outlet returning seven times on marginal capacity and a backlog still covering several years. That is a good business and the price would be defensible. Every one of those four conditions has to hold.
