Section 02

Business model and value chain

MTAR buys specialty metal, machines and fabricates it to somebody else's drawing to extremely tight tolerance, and ships it. It does not own a design, a brand or an end customer in any segment except nuclear. Everything else in this report follows from that.

The company in one paragraph

MTAR Technologies is a Hyderabad precision engineering company founded in 1970 to make components the state sector could not. It runs seven-plus divisions and, on a recent recruitment posting, more than 600 computer-controlled machine tools, employing about 2,562 people. It takes customer drawings and converts stainless steel, superalloys, titanium and sheet metal into critical assemblies. More than 70% of revenue is earned on export orders. Its single largest customer is an American fuel cell maker estimated at 55-65% of revenue.

A disclosure fact that governs this whole section

Every quarterly and annual filing states that MTAR operates in one operating segment under Indian accounting standard 108. The four-way split below appears only in investor presentations and the management discussion, carries no auditor assurance, no consistency requirement and no restatement-disclosure obligation. That is the structural reason the segment numbers in Section 6 do not reconcile across documents.

Unknown unknowns in this segment
  • The relationship is asymmetric in disclosure. MTAR discloses this customer as the majority of its revenue. No accessible customer filing, call or release names MTAR anywhere. It is existential for one party and unremarkable for the other.
  • The customer states it has multiple qualified suppliers. On its own Q2 2026 call: "multiple qualified suppliers across multiple countries for every critical input", "no single supplier determining their operations". That was said rebutting a raw-materials allegation and may be about inputs rather than sub-assembly partners. It may equally be exactly what it says.
  • The capacity arithmetic does not obviously work. On an analyst's ratio of about 15,400 hot boxes per gigawatt, a 2 GW customer needs roughly 30,800 units a year. MTAR's FY28 capacity of 30,000 covers essentially all of a 2 GW customer - but only about 40% of the 5 GW the customer says its footprint can support. If the customer goes to 5 GW, MTAR cannot mathematically hold a majority share without a step-change it has not disclosed.
  • Moving down the value chain, not up. Enclosures, sheet metal and cable harnesses are lower value-added than precision machining. Revenue accretive, margin dilutive, and the gross margin trail confirms it.
  • Pricing power is not theoretical. A three-and-a-half week tariff standoff with this customer cost MTAR a quarter of earnings in Q2 FY26. On that same call the Managing Director said "our operating margins are at 40%" - disclosing his own gross margin, and the size of the prize, to his customer's procurement team.

Unknown unknowns in this segment
  • The gatekeeper problem. For Kaiga, MTAR sells through Megha Engineering, not to NPCIL. For Mahi Banswara the Managing Director confirmed participation would again be through an EPC vendor. The intermediary owns the customer relationship, the schedule and a share of the margin.
  • A 28.8x book-to-revenue ratio. ₹684 cr of backlog against ₹23.6 cr of FY26 revenue, with execution not yet begun and the dedicated facility still "being prepared". No nuclear quality-assurance or code-stamp hiring was found in any job posting.
  • Nothing here is a small modular reactor. Despite the narrative, no small modular or Bharat Small Reactor contract exists in any record. Today's nuclear revenue is conventional heavy-water reactor supply and refurbishment.
  • The order book was flat while the book doubled. Nuclear went from ₹679.0 cr to ₹684.0 cr in the record quarter, and from 26.3% to 13.3% of the book.

Products and others, plus data centre infrastructure

Unknown unknowns in this segment
  • The residual bucket is doing the work. Products and others shrank 9.1% in FY26 to ₹134.1 cr, then delivered ₹100.4 cr in Q1 FY27 alone - 75% of the entire prior year in one quarter, and 27.8% of revenue, the highest share in the series. An "and others" bucket is the natural home for anything that does not fit elsewhere.
  • It may be more of the same customer. Management confirmed on a call that air separation assemblies go to the fuel cell customer at about 125,000 units a quarter, roughly sixteen per hot box, and that enclosures went from 500 to 1,000 units a quarter. Asked directly whether the products line is fuel-cell content, the answer was "No, not necessarily. We have the other products as well." Best estimate: true customer-linked revenue in Q1 FY27 is plausibly 70-80%, above the 61% headline.
  • The data centre line is ₹45 cr and 100% export, to an unnamed energy-sector entity, with the products never described in any filing. Management: "still in its early stages... meaningful ramp-up not expected until the next year." Not a reported order-book segment.

The customer earns more after its operating costs than its critical supplier earns before them

Bloom Energy's operating margin of 22.5% exceeds MTAR's EBITDA margin of 21.1%. MTAR captures roughly 3% of its principal customer's revenue, and on the company's own FY27 guidance that ratio does not change. The direction of travel in product mix - from precision machining toward enclosures, sheet metal and cable harnesses - is revenue accretive and margin dilutive, which is exactly what a 380 basis point gross margin decline over two years looks like.

MTAR is becoming a larger, more indispensable, lower-gross-margin sub-system assembler to one dominant customer. The EBITDA improvement is a volume-leverage story with a finite ceiling, not a value-capture story.
