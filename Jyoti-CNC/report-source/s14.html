Section 14

Valuation

At ₹1,061 the market pays 75 times trailing earnings and 47 times EBITDA for a company whose incremental capital has earned less than its cost in the latest year. The reverse DCF says the price needs revenue to compound at about a third a year for a decade while capital turns roughly twice as fast as it ever has. No growth rate reaches the price at the capital intensity actually achieved.

Choice of multiple. Free cash flow has been negative in every year since listing, so price to free cash flow and a conventional forward DCF anchored on cash are unusable. P/E on normalised earnings is the primary multiple, cross-checked with EV/EBITDA against the global builders. Both are flattered by revenue recognised ahead of billing (Section 6), which a cash-based measure would not be.

Reverse DCF first: what the price already assumes

Method: free cash flow to the firm = operating EBIT after 25.17% tax, less reinvestment; reinvestment = the year's revenue increase divided by a sales-to-capital ratio (rupees of new revenue per rupee of new capital). Ten years at a constant growth rate, a five-year fade to 5% terminal growth, and a terminal value that reinvests at the implied return. ! What the central case requires

At a 12.5% cost of capital and the FY26 margin, the price requires about 36% a year revenue growth for ten years with capital turning at 1.0, against 0.42-0.63 achieved. That is revenue of about ₹45,000 cr in FY36, 22 times FY26 and more than the entire Indian machine-tool market today (₹36,419 cr consumption in FY26, IMTMA). At management's own guidance of 25-30% growth sustained for ten years, the price is reached only if growth needs almost no capital; at 20% growth, not even zero reinvestment reaches it (value about ₹17,500 cr). The market is not paying for the business Jyoti has run since listing. It is paying for a much larger one that converts its sales to cash far better.

Are those assumptions achievable, given Sections 1-13? The growth half is not impossible for a few years: the order book, the new plant and the defence and electronics demand in Section 20 support 20-30% for a period. The capital half is where the evidence runs the other way. Working capital has absorbed 86% of revenue at FY26, unbilled revenue has trebled, customer advances cover under 2% of the order book, and the new plant is designed around EMS volume machines whose customers have not taken delivery of two-year-old orders. Nothing in the record shows capital turning faster; FY26 showed it turning slower.

The swing assumption between bear and base is not the Indian machine business, which is growing on any reading. It is two binary-looking items: the outcome of the French investigation (and with it Huron's licences, ₹882 cr of parent exposure and ₹264 cr of guarantees), and whether the ₹613 cr unbilled balance turns into invoices and cash. At ₹1,061 the price sits above the base case and about two-thirds of the way from bear to bull.
