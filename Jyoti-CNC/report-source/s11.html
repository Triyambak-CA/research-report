External headwinds and tailwinds

Jyoti's earnings are most sensitive to three outside variables: the euro, because its balance sheet is long euro through the French subsidiary and unhedged; interest rates, because all of its ₹846 cr of debt is floating; and the pace at which Indian manufacturers, and the government, spend on capital goods.

Global linkages

Headwinds

  • Export controls and the Russia allegation. The dual-use regime is now the single largest external variable for Huron's revenue: about ₹100 cr on 7-8 machines for Chinese and Turkish customers awaits licences, and the Chairman called Huron's export markets "all our sensitive areas" (Aug-2026).
  • China. The largest and fastest-rising source of Indian machine-tool imports (30% in FY26), competing at the volume end where Jyoti's unit growth is concentrated.
  • Falling tariffs on EU and US machines. The India-US joint statement (Feb-2026) and the India-EU agreement lower tariffs on imported industrial goods; the machinery safety order that would have slowed imports was rescinded in Jan-2026.
  • Global machine-tool downcycle. Production outside India fell in 2024; Japanese and German builders have a strong reason to push into India.

Tailwinds

  • US tariffs matter little directly. Management said on 07-Aug-2025 that Jyoti had no US export sales that quarter and exports mainly to the euro zone. US tariffs on Indian goods (18% agreed Feb-2026; a 10% Section 301 duty from 24-Jul-2026) therefore hit customers more than Jyoti.
  • China-plus-one electronics capex in India, subsidised by ECMS, is the stated source of the EMS book and of 70-80% of the new plant's intended use.
  • Defence indigenisation with domestic-reserved procurement and record domestic defence production.
  • Domestic capital goods cycle in its mid-phase: IIP capital goods +8.3% FY26, capacity utilisation 74.8%.

What a clean euro move does, and why it matters twice

Because the parent is long euro and the subsidiary borrows in euro, a stronger euro raises reported profit (revaluation of the parent's euro claims on Huron, booked in other income) while also raising the rupee value of Huron's debt (booked in OCI). FY26 is the illustration: ₹43.39 cr of forex gain in profit, ₹18.01 cr of translation loss in OCI. If those intra-group claims are part of the net investment in Huron, as CARO's "no stipulation of repayment" suggests, the gain arguably belongs in OCI too (Section 6). A reversal of the euro would take back in profit what FY26 gave, as Q1 FY27 began to show.
