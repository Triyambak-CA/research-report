Section 17

Capex deployment and return on capex

Machine tools are cheap to make capacity for and expensive to sell. Jyoti's new 10,000-machine plant costs about ₹4.5 lakh of fixed capital per machine of annual capacity; every machine sold ties up roughly seven times that in working capital. The return on growth here is set by the balance sheet between the factory and the customer, not by the factory.

The three-year number is respectable because it starts from a depressed base. The last year is the warning: the company added ₹656 cr of capital in FY26 and earned ₹22 cr more operating profit on it. Part of that is timing, because the new plant was built but not yet producing and Huron was hit by the investigation; part of it is the working-capital model, which took ₹309 cr in a year when revenue grew ₹275 cr. Compared with a cost of equity of about 13% (assumed) and a post-tax return on the whole of operating capital of about 15% in FY26, FY26's increment was value-destroying on its face.

Capital-allocation verdict

Jyoti has a reinvestment outlet: demand is real and the core business earns its keep. It does not have a self-funding one. Since listing it has reinvested more than 100% of its earnings, borrowed the difference, paid no dividend and bought no shares, and in its most recent year earned almost nothing on the increment. The question is not whether it should be distributing (it cannot) but whether it can shorten the cycle between building a machine and being paid for it. Management's FY27 target of operating cash flow at "close to 50% of EBITDA" is the test. Until it is met, every rupee of growth is financed by the balance sheet, and the 10,000-machine plant makes that dependence larger, not smaller.
