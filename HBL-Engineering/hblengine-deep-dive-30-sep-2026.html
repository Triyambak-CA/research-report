HBL Engineering

NSE: HBLENGINE · BSE: 517271 · 30-Sep-2026 · Data as of Q1 FY27 (quarter ended 30-Jun-2026) · Price ₹808.30 (29-Sep-2026 close)

A Hyderabad maker of specialised batteries, founded in 1977, that became, for one year, India's leading supplier of Kavach train-collision avoidance systems, and trebled its profit doing it. The question this report tries to answer is how much of FY26 was a delivery peak, how much of it the refilled 2026 order book can repeat, and what is left when Kavach competition arrives.

Executive summary

HBL is two businesses sharing one balance sheet. One is a durable, 25%-margin specialised battery and defence franchise worth perhaps a third of today's price. The other is Kavach, which took FY26 revenue from Rs 1,967 cr to Rs 3,303 cr and profit from Rs 277 cr to Rs 815 cr, mostly on one locomotive order that HBL delivered three-quarters of before the rest was cancelled. The order book has since been refilled with about Rs 2,980 cr of new on-board orders, and management now says FY27 will be "significantly better", six months after calling FY26 "exceptional". The price, 27.5 times FY26 profit, is roughly fair if that is true and stays true after FY29; it is not cheap if Kavach behaves like the multi-vendor, price-tendered programme it has become.

The three strongest positives

  1. HBL executes Kavach better than anyone else. It pioneered the system with RDSO, was first to Version 4.0 approval (13-May-2025), and delivered 1,659 of the roughly 3,000 locomotive sets all five suppliers managed in the 2024 round. It then won about Rs 2,980 cr of new on-board orders in 2026 plus Rs 1,300 cr of trackside work. Official scope already sanctioned is worth about Rs 17,800 cr unspent; the pipeline does not run out by FY29.
  2. A real, qualified battery franchise under the Kavach story. Rs 1,416 cr of industrial batteries at about 25% segment margin (the world's second-largest industrial NiCad maker by its own claim, India's only PLT maker), NiCad at 90% utilisation, exports up, data-centre PLT sales doubling; plus defence batteries for aircraft, missiles, torpedoes and submarines.
  3. A clean balance sheet and an undiluted, unpledged promoter. Net cash about Rs 757 cr, CARE A+/Positive, debt cut from Rs 686 cr to Rs 67 cr over a decade, no equity issued in over ten years, promoter never a seller. Cash conversion was 93% of operating profit before tax in FY26, and tax was paid in full.

The three sharpest risks

  1. Kavach has become a price-tendered, multi-vendor market. HBL won nothing in CLW's 6,300-unit tender in Jan-2026 "because other bidder's prices were lower"; Kernex holds a larger CLW order; two more vendors are near approval. Management guides Kavach sales and profit down from FY29, and every 2026 order carries the same twelve-month forfeiture terms that cost HBL 541 sets.
  2. The new businesses have never hit a date. Rs 440 cr of drivetrain sales guided for FY24-FY26, none delivered; sales dates moved three times; fuze approvals three years late; torpedo batteries written off (Rs 26.49 cr); the e-truck subsidiary being struck off. The price needs at least one of them to become a second Kavach.
  3. Governance and reporting. A trust voted by one family director carried the related-party and 2026 appointment resolutions against 86-98% public-institution opposition; the CMD's re-appointment on 5% commission, decided by public votes alone, passed with 75.30% against a 75% threshold; the FY24 annual report states his commission two ways, Rs 15.28 cr apart, never corrected; the audited P&L shows associates' profit on a basis its own note contradicts; a company HBL said it had "incorporated" to take two of its product lines is now owned 50:50 by two family directors; the Jun-2025 shareholding pattern repeats March to the share; and a Kavach forecast made ten weeks before year-end appears to have been missed by at least Rs 254 cr (the letter does not state its GST basis).

The one line

This is a bet on Kavach being a decade-long national programme in which HBL keeps a leading share at today's margins while a second business reaches revenue, priced as if FY26's peak profit is a floor that grows about 18% a year for ten years, and it breaks if the 2026 orders are delivered late or at lower margins, or if FY27 fails to beat FY26.

Industry and sector analysis

HBL sells into three industries with very different cycles. Railway safety electronics is a government programme in its early, fast-rising phase, where the constraint is approvals and budget, not demand. Specialised batteries are a mature, slow-growing market being reshaped by lithium-ion. Defence electronics and ammunition components are in a funded upcycle driven by indigenisation and post-Operation Sindoor procurement. HBL has commissioned no industry report (it is not an IPO); the numbers below are official where possible, with one competitor-commissioned forecast used and flagged.

Cycle position: early upcycle, supply-constrained by approvals. Kavach has been the national automatic train protection standard since Jul-2020, but orders stopped from Feb-2022 until Aug-2024 while Version 4.0 was specified (16-Jul-2024) and approved vendor by vendor (HBL 13-May-2025, Kernex 14-Oct-2025). Since then awards have come in waves: the 2024 tender for 10,000 locomotive sets, of which only about 3,000 were delivered in time; 2026 tenders for about 11,400 more; trackside bids for about 15,000 route km. The budget line is rising 23% a year. On official unit costs, the scope already sanctioned is worth about Rs 21,700 cr, of which about Rs 17,800 cr is unspent; full-network coverage would be about Rs 45,500 cr. At the FY27 allocation the sanctioned scope lasts about nine years; at the Mar-Jun 2026 pace, about five. The pipeline does not end in FY29; management's FY29 caution is about competition and price, not demand.

Issuer-commissioned industry source, used with a flag

The only published market forecast is CareEdge Research's automatic train protection report of 06-Dec-2024, "exclusively commissioned and paid for" by Quadrant Future Tek for its IPO prospectus (RHP 27-Dec-2024; commissioned by a competitor of HBL, not by HBL). It puts the Indian ATP market at USD 134 mn in CY24, rising every year to USD 384 mn in CY30 and USD 420 mn in CY31: about USD 1.78 bn (roughly Rs 16,700 cr) over CY24-CY30, with no decline forecast. That is the same order as the official-cost value of the scope already sanctioned but unspent (about Rs 17,800 cr), which is the independent counterweight. A Kavach integrator's investor deck (E2E Transportation, May-2026) claims a Rs 1.5-2 lakh cr market, which no official figure supports. HBL's own contract prices sit below the ministry's unit costs: Rs 69.2 lakh a locomotive on the 2024 CLW order and about Rs 22-32 lakh a route km on its 2025 trackside orders, against Rs 80 lakh and Rs 50 lakh, so official-cost market sizes overstate the vendor revenue pool.

Structural shifts. (1) From a three-vendor oligopoly to five or more: Quadrant Future Tek, G G Tronics (CG Power) and Progota India are at or near approval. (2) From on-board to trackside: the next wave is route km, which is slower, involves EPC partners and civil work, and carries longer defect-liability periods. (3) From Kavach to traffic management (TMS/CTC), where HBL claims under 2% of the network is covered today (unverified). Value capture: the design owner and integrator (HBL, Kernex) captures most of the margin; radios, processors and cabling are bought in; the railway, as sole buyer and specifier, sets price by tender and has now shown it will switch vendors on price.

2. Specialised and industrial batteries

Size and growth (secondary, labelled): India's industrial battery market is estimated at about USD 758 mn in 2024 growing about 5% a year (IMARC, paid research vendor, page not opened); lead-acid overall about USD 4.6 bn (2025). India's lithium-ion import bill rose from about USD 384 mn (FY19) to over USD 3 bn (FY25) (IEEFA, secondary). HBL's own claim: UPS batteries grow about 6% a year. No official NiCad market size or import series exists; NITI Aayog's Trade Watch carries no battery line. Cycle position: mature. Telecom has moved from lead-acid to lithium-ion, leaving "excess Industry capacity for 2V-VRLA batteries" (HBL MD&A); NiCad demand is supported by oil and gas, power, metros and locomotive pantographs, and HBL runs its NiCad plant at 90%.

Regulatory architecture: Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022 (extended producer responsibility, minimum recycled content from FY28; 3,664 producers registered); a quality control order now covers storage batteries for solar systems (IS 16270:2023); PLI for advanced chemistry cells (Rs 18,100 cr, 40 GWh awarded, 1 GWh installed, "domestic demand continues to be met largely through imports"). HBL is outside the PLI and says it will make cells only for defence. Input prices (measured): LME lead averaged USD 1,952/t in FY26, 7.9% below FY24; nickel USD 15,601/t, 18.6% below FY24, then up 12.5% in FY27 so far; cadmium about USD 3.90/kg in 2025 (USGS). The rupee fell about 10% in FY26 (secondary), which offset part of the dollar decline.

3. Defence electronics, batteries and fuzes

Funded upcycle. Defence capital outlay rises from Rs 1,80,000 cr (FY26 BE) to Rs 2,19,306 cr (FY27 BE, +21.8%), with 75% of capital acquisition reserved for domestic sources; the Ministry cites emergency procurement "subsequent to Operation Sindoor". Defence production reached Rs 1.78 lakh cr in FY26 (+15.6%; private sector about 24%) and exports Rs 38,424 cr (PIB). The Defence Acquisition Council granted Rs 6.73 lakh cr of acceptances of necessity in FY26. For HBL's niches, nothing in the official record is specific: no fuze or battery item appears on the fifth positive indigenisation list (OCR of the annexure), and ammunition-fuze procurement volumes are not published. Two incumbents already supply electronic fuzes at scale: Bharat Electronics (a ten-year, Rs 5,336 cr contract, media) and ECIL, which says it has supplied "over 2.2 million" electronic fuzes to the Army over 25 years.

4. Electric trucks and marine propulsion

Both are pre-commercial. PM E-DRIVE (Rs 10,900 cr, extended to Mar-2028) sets aside Rs 500 cr for e-trucks up to 55 t, paying up to Rs 9.3 lakh a truck in HBL's weight band; as of 19-Mar-2026 not a rupee had been paid for an e-truck, and the ministry calls the market "still in a nascent phase". In marine, the Green Tug Transition Programme targets 16 green tugs in phase 1 (2024-2027) and 50 by 2030; Cochin Shipyard holds an order for two battery-electric tugs with "batteries ... sourced domestically" (PIB 31-Jan-2026; HBL is not named).

Where India sits and who captures value

India is building an indigenous automatic train protection standard rather than importing Europe's ETCS, which is why every approved Kavach vendor so far is Indian (report's reading). In batteries India is an importer of cells and an exporter of niche industrial chemistries; HBL's NiCad exports to 59 countries are the global position. In defence, value sits with platform DPSUs; component makers like HBL capture margin only where they own qualification.

Business model and value chain

HBL is a Hyderabad engineering company, founded in 1977 and incorporated in 1983 (it changed its name from HBL Power Systems in November 2024), that exists to make things India otherwise imports. It earns money three ways: it sells specialised batteries to industry, railways and exporters; it sells railway safety electronics, above all Kavach, to Indian Railways; and it sells batteries and electronics to the armed forces. A fourth line, electric drivetrains for heavy trucks and boats, has been in development for a decade and has no revenue.

Unknown unknowns

? Dependencies a first read would miss
  • One buyer, one approver. Kavach revenue depends on one ministry buying and one standards body (RDSO) approving each version. Version 3.2 to 4.0 cost the industry a year; the next version change could do the same. HBL now also carries upgrade contracts (Ver 3.2 to 4.0) on its own earlier installations.
  • Forfeiture, not penalty. The CLW contract did not merely charge liquidated damages; it cancelled undelivered sets. Twelve-month on-board terms and HBL's 75% on-time record together make every new order a partial option.
  • Warranty tail. Rs 92.75 cr provided for Kavach defect-liability warranty, to be spent over 12-96 months; field failures on a safety system would cost more than money.
  • Bank guarantees. Rs 303 cr of performance guarantees outstanding at 31-Mar-2026, callable on non-performance.
  • Input metals and China. Cadmium and nickel are exchange-priced and HBL does not disclose hedging or pass-through clauses; lithium cells for every Li-ion product, including the truck drivetrain, come from China, the same supply chain that just cost the drivetrain nine months over magnets.
  • Family control of the vote. Institutional shareholders vote against almost every family and related-party resolution; they pass on the promoter trust's votes (Section 7). Capital allocation is the family's call.
  • Associate capital. Money is flowing into associates (Tonbo Imaging, Naval Systems & Technologies, Xalten Systems) and a Category II AIF, none of which the operating business needs.

Two readings follow. First, price competition has arrived, earlier than management's FY29 guide. HBL's own letter of 15-Jan-2026: "From the CLW loco Kavach tender for 6,300 units, decided this week, HBL did not get any order, because other bidder's prices were lower." Kernex's 3,024 sets and G G Tronics' order from CLW in the same fortnight appear to be that tender. Headline per-set prices still look close to HBL's 2024 price (Kernex Rs 69.1 lakh ex GST if its figure includes tax; Quadrant Rs 67.6 lakh), so the undercut was probably a few percent, but it was enough to shut HBL out of the largest round of 2026. Second, share has moved: in the 2024 round HBL delivered 1,659 of about 3,000 sets supplied by five vendors (its own estimate, 18-Dec-2025). Batteries and defence

In batteries HBL is a niche player beside two scale leaders: Exide and Amara Raja earn 10-11% EBITDA margins on Rs 13,500-18,000 cr of sales; HBL's battery segment earns about 25% on Rs 1,416 cr because it lives in NiCad ("second largest supplier ... globally"), PLT (the only Indian maker), aviation and defence chemistries where volumes are small and qualification is slow. The comparable niche business, High Energy Batteries, shows the downside of that niche without growth: flat sales for three years and margins falling from 37% to 21%. The threat named in HBL's own MD&A is lithium-ion substitution: telecom has already moved, creating "excess Industry capacity for 2V-VRLA batteries" and "pricing pressure extending even to remaining application sectors".

In defence electronics HBL is sub-scale (Rs 212 cr of segment revenue) against listed peers valued at 46-123 times earnings. The fuze market has a scale incumbent: Bharat Electronics signed a ten-year, Rs 5,336 cr electronic-fuze contract with the Ministry of Defence in Dec-2023 (media report; MoD release not opened). HBL's claim that "there is no other Indian company with this capability" in electronic fuzes sits awkwardly beside BEL's contract and Apollo's DRDO technology transfer for a grenade fuze.

Section 04

Competitive advantage: the 6 Cs

HBL's durable advantage is qualification: it has spent five decades getting products approved by buyers who rarely approve anyone, from aircraft batteries in 1977 to Kavach with RDSO. That is a real moat in batteries and defence, where it protects about Rs 1,600 cr of revenue at 25-35% margins. In Kavach the same advantage bought HBL a head start, and a head start is what it is now spending.

Cost Moderate

In-house design of everything except cells and commodity metals (Kavach software, BMS, fuze components, motors); plants in low-cost Telangana and Andhra Pradesh. Gross margin 49% (FY25) and 58% (FY26), well above Exide and Amara Raja (EBITDA 10-11%), but that reflects niche mix, not a structural cost edge in the same products: in lead-acid HBL runs at 54% utilisation against excess industry capacity. No disclosed hedging of nickel, cadmium or lead; CARE says pass-through comes "with a time lag".

Capacity Moderate

NiCad at 90% utilisation and being expanded; PLT capacity being doubled; the only Indian PLT line and, by its own claim, the world's second-largest industrial NiCad maker. A defence lithium cell plant (about Rs 200 cr with R&D) and a licensed fuze factory are built ahead of demand. Kavach needs little capacity: the constraint in 2025 was testing and installation labour on locomotives, and HBL still missed a quarter of its order. A new Kavach entrant can build assembly capacity in months; approval is the barrier, not capacity.

Capital Strong

Net cash of about Rs 757 cr, CARE A+/Positive and A1+, debt Rs 67 cr, bank lines at Rs 798.75 cr of which Rs 551 cr is for guarantees. In a railway or defence procurement cycle, where guarantees and working capital decide who can bid, a clean balance sheet is a weapon: it let HBL finance the CLW order with a project-specific Rs 350 cr line and then surrender it. The weakness is behavioural: surplus is flowing into associates and an AIF rather than back to shareholders (payout about 10%).

Channel Weak

There is no channel in the usual sense. HBL sells by tender to railways and defence, through EPC contractors on trackside Kavach (Shivakriti, IRCON, Ashoka Buildcon), and through OEMs (Cummins white-label PLT, Siemens Germany Li-ion development). It has no distribution to fall back on if a tender is lost, and no evidence of brand pull. Exports (59 countries, Rs 520 cr) are real but project-based. For the drivetrain it has no dealer or service network at all.

Customer captivity Moderate

High in defence and aviation batteries: qualified products stay qualified for a platform's life, and replacement demand recurs ("Batteries are 'revenue' items"). Low in Kavach on-board: the buyer is one ministry that deliberately qualifies several vendors, allocates by tender and cancels late deliveries. Somewhat higher in trackside, where a vendor's installed section needs its own upgrades (HBL's Ver 3.2 to 4.0 upgrade contracts) and maintenance. Concentration is extreme: railways were 53% of FY26 standalone revenue.

Compliance moat Strong

First vendor approved on Kavach Ver 4.0 (13-May-2025); RDSO approvals for signalling; defence qualifications across aircraft, missile, torpedo and submarine batteries; DRDO cell-technology licence; MHA approval for the grenade fuze and a licensed fuze factory. These took years each. The limit is that approvals are not exclusive: Kernex cleared Ver 4.0 five months later, and two more vendors are in final trials. The compliance moat delays rivals rather than excluding them.

The most important moat, and its half-life

Qualification in defence and specialised industrial batteries. It is the part of HBL that earned 23-37% segment margins before Kavach (FY25: batteries 22.9%, defence 37.4%) and that no competitor has dislodged in decades. Its half-life is long, perhaps ten years or more, eroded mainly by chemistry change (lithium-ion replacing lead-acid, as it already has in telecom) rather than by rivals. The Kavach advantage has a much shorter half-life: HBL moved from sole practical supplier (more than half of 2024 deliveries) to about half of the 2026 awards in eighteen months, and management's own guide (decline from FY29) implies three to four years. The market is paying for the Kavach advantage as if it had the batteries' half-life.

Durability badges are the report's judgement on the evidence cited; the Ratings control hides them.

HBL is a founder-run family company in which the founder's daughter alone votes 51.3% of the shares through the family trust. The family has never sold a share, never pledged one and never diluted anyone, which is rare and valuable. From FY24 the board stopped paying below the approved rate: commission went from about 3% of the base to exactly the 5% cap in the year profits trebled. The commission is uncapped in rupees and runs to 2030.
  • The FY24 annual report gives the CMD's FY24 commission two ways: Rs 19.06 cr in the Board's report, Rs 3.78 cr (the FY23 figure) in the corporate governance report and its annexure: a Rs 15.28 cr inconsistency in two of the four places a reader would look, in the year the pay quadrupled, never corrected (the FY25 AGM notice gives Rs 19.04 cr). Only the commission cell is stale: the same table updates the salary. The auditor's governance certificate did not catch it.

Tonbo Imaging: the largest capital decision of the period

In Feb-2023 the board approved up to Rs 150 cr in compulsorily convertible preference shares of Tonbo Imaging India (thermal and electro-optic imaging for defence), for about 35% on conversion, and told the exchange the deal "would not fall within the definition of related party transactions" and that HBL had no shareholding in Tonbo. Five months earlier HBL and Tonbo had formed a 51:49 JV with Kavita Prasad Aluru as a director; the filing did not mention it. HBL paid Rs 86.67 cr by Sep-2023 (screener's "Rs 87 cr acquisition of preference shares") and then stopped: "a higher valuation was expected by Tonbo for the balance, which was not accepted by HBL" (07-Feb-2024). On conversion in Mar-2025 it received 11.13%, now 14.25% after a bonus and split; the implied value of Tonbo rose from about Rs 430 cr on the original terms to about Rs 780 cr on the stake received. In FY24 HBL sold about Rs 68.85 cr of goods to its associates and bought Rs 41.68 cr (screener's "Rs 42 cr purchases"), almost all Tonbo; associate trade was nil in FY25 and FY26. The H1 FY24 related-party statement omitted Tonbo although the money had gone, and Tonbo was classified as an associate only at year end, which let a full year of its profit be booked in Q4 FY24. The auditor has noted for three years that an inventory-policy difference at Tonbo "could not be ascertained". None of this is shown to be improper: Tonbo's revenue held at Rs 370-474 cr after HBL's trade stopped, which argues against round-tripping. It is, however, a related-party investment disclosed narrowly, bought at a rising price, and now reportedly heading for an IPO that HBL has not mentioned (Section 10).

Moebius Power Electronics and other promoter-linked companies

! A company "incorporated by HBL" that two family directors now own

On 11-Nov-2022 HBL's board approved buying 100% of Moebius Power Electronics Pvt Ltd at face value (Rs 1 lakh); in its reply to NSE HBL said the deal was a related-party transaction and that none of Moebius's activities "are / would be outside the main line of activity of HBL" (power electronics, EV chargers, inverters, energy storage). In Jun-2023 the board considered selling two HBL product lines (power electronics and integrated power supplies for railways, Rs 67 cr of FY23 sales) to Moebius "wherein HBL remain as a shareholder"; on 28-Jun-2023 it found the proposal "not attractive" and rejected it, and the note filed that day says Moebius "was ... incorporated by HBL on July 15, 2022 ... The initial subscribers and first Directors ... were Mrs. Kavita Prasad and Mr. MSS Srinath, representing HBL." Whether Moebius now trades in HBL's product lines is unverified (no company-registry filings on file). The board's rejection of the slump sale is the one clear instance of the independent process working.

! Governance verdict

Aligned on the balance sheet (no pledge, no dilution, falling debt, a buying promoter), misaligned on the income statement (a commission at the statutory cap that outruns the public dividend) and on voice (the public is outvoted on the contested related-party and 2026 appointment resolutions by a trust one family director votes, under a counting method that changed between 2025 and 2026). Industry-specific regulatory dependencies

  • RDSO type approval. Kavach is sold to a specification (RDSO/SPN/196/2020) that changes by version. The Version 4.0 specification was approved on 16-Jul-2024 and HBL's product on 13-May-2025; FY25 revenue fell 12% partly because of that wait (CARE: "delay in RDSO approval"). Every version change is a revenue gap for the incumbent and an entry window for rivals.
  • Contract terms. Railway on-board contracts forfeit undelivered quantities at the deadline. HBL lost 541 sets (about Rs 374 cr) this way in Dec-2025. Trackside contracts carry a defect-liability period, for which HBL provided Rs 92.75 cr in FY26.
  • Battery rules. Extended Producer Responsibility under the Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022 obliges HBL to collect and recycle a share of batteries sold (EPR provision Rs 17.37 cr at 31-Mar-2026). Lead is a hazardous input; CARE flags "stringent pollution control regulations". HBL cites zero liquid discharge for two decades.
  • Defence qualification. Fuzes must be proof-fired on real weapons at declared ranges; the Army and 155 mm approvals are expected "during 2027". Timing is controlled by range availability, not by HBL. A licensed fuze factory is required and has been built.
  • Vehicle homologation. The truck drivetrain needs approval (expected Mar-2027); marine drives need classification-society certification (sales "limited until end of September 2027").

  • Kavach is the national automatic train protection system (adopted Jul-2020), with a rising budget line: Rs 1,112.57 cr (FY25), Rs 1,673.19 cr (FY26), Rs 2,066 cr (FY27 BE) (PIB, Lok Sabha replies).
  • Make in India preference in defence ("Defence has a strong purchase preference for Made In India", AR FY26), and the positive indigenisation lists.
  • Lithium-ion cell import costs have risen (Chinese prices, freight, rupee), which slows the substitution of HBL's lead-acid products (AR FY26 MD&A).

Section 14

Valuation

At Rs 808 (29-Sep-2026 close) HBL is priced at Rs 22,406 cr: 27.5 times FY26 profit and about 51 times the latest quarter annualised. The reverse DCF says the price needs FY26's profit to be a floor that then grows about 18% a year for ten years. On management's own FY27-FY28 view (better than FY26, then a Kavach fade from FY29), the next two years roughly earn the price; the decade after them has to be carried by businesses that do not yet have revenue.

The right multiple

Price to earnings on a normalised year is the primary measure, because HBL is net cash (Rs 824 cr of cash and treasury against Rs 67 cr of debt), carries little fixed capital in its largest profit line, and has earnings that swing by a factor of three with one customer's delivery schedule. EV/EBITDA adds nothing that P/E misses here, and price to book (10.1x) is inflated by a year's retained windfall. Trailing P/E is misleading in both directions: it looks cheap (19th percentile of its own five-year range) because the denominator is a delivery peak, while the latest quarter annualised makes it look expensive because most of the 2026 orders had not yet been delivered.

Pre-mortem and anti-thesis

Assume it is Sep-2028 and the stock has halved. This is the most likely story of how that happened, argued as a short-seller would argue it.

The short case, argued sincerely

  1. One order made the year, and it is gone. Up to about Rs 1,148 cr of FY26 revenue, as much as 35% of the company, came from one CLW contract that ended in December 2025, a quarter of it forfeited. The 2026 replacement orders are twelve-month contracts on the same forfeiture terms, won against more competitors, one of whom has already beaten HBL on price.
  2. The moat was a head start, and it has been spent. HBL delivered more than half of all on-board sets in the 2024 round; it lost the largest 2026 round outright. Kernex now has a bigger CLW order, G G Tronics and Quadrant are close to approval, and the buyer deliberately multi-sources. Management's own guide has Kavach profit declining from FY29, and its guide has already moved once.
  3. The rest of the company is a 20-25% margin battery maker growing at single digits, facing lithium substitution in lead-acid and flat defence revenue. On FY25-FY26 numbers, batteries plus defence earn about Rs 400-420 cr of segment profit. At a battery multiple that is worth a third of today's price.
  4. The new businesses are a decade of dates. Rs 440 cr of drivetrain sales guided for FY24-FY26, none delivered; three sales dates missed; the drivetrain subsidiary being struck off; fuze approvals three years late; torpedo batteries written off. The 0% on-time record for new verticals is the base rate to apply to "substantial sources of sales and profits in a few years" (24-May-2026 letter).
  5. Governance is family control with the public outvoted. Public institutions vote 86-100% against family and related-party resolutions; most pass on the trust's votes, which one family director casts. The CMD's commission rose from Rs 18.95 cr to Rs 56.02 cr, and his re-appointment on 5% of profits, the one family resolution the public decided alone, passed with 75.30% against a 75% threshold. Surplus goes to associates, an AIF and a thermal-imaging stake bought at a price HBL itself later refused to pay more of.
  6. The numbers are not handled with care. Associates' profit shown gross of tax on the audited P&L face and net in the note, for three years; two versions of FY25 segment assets; results filed in a non-compliant format; a Jun-2025 shareholding pattern identical to March; a Kavach forecast ten weeks before year-end apparently missed by at least 13%, and never explained. No analyst has ever been able to ask about any of it on a call.
  7. Nobody who could defend the price owns it. No active mutual fund holds the stock; FPIs are 5.6%; the register is 3.5 lakh retail holders and index funds. When FY27 disappoints, there is no institutional buyer of last resort.

Section 16

Investment pitch: both sides

No rating and no target. Two cases, each argued as its advocate would argue it to an investment committee, and one line that states the bet.

The 90-second case to own it

HBL is the company that built Kavach with the railways and delivered more of it than anyone: 1,659 locomotive sets in 2025, more than every other vendor combined on its own estimate. The network is barely started: 2,633 of about 69,000 route km commissioned on Version 4.0, Rs 2,066 cr budgeted this year and rising, and a national mandate behind it. HBL has refilled its order book with about Rs 2,980 cr of new on-board orders and Rs 1,300 cr of trackside work, and management says FY27 will be "significantly better" than a year in which it earned Rs 815 cr. Underneath sits a 25%-margin specialised battery business, the world's second-largest industrial NiCad maker and India's only PLT maker, with data-centre and export demand ahead of capacity. It is net cash (about Rs 757 cr), rated A+/Positive, and has spent about Rs 200 cr building defence cell and fuze capacity that pays off if two approvals due in 2027 arrive. No active fund owns it, so a second growth leg would be discovered, not already priced. At about 25 times FY27 earnings on management's view, the market is paying for Kavach, not for the options.

Horizon: 2-3 years, through FY28, with the fuze approvals and the truck launch as the second-leg evidence.

The 90-second case to exit or avoid

The year that re-rated HBL was one order, and a quarter of that order was cancelled because HBL was late. Since then it has lost the largest 2026 round on price, Kernex has a bigger CLW book, two more vendors are near approval, and management guides Kavach down from FY29. Strip Kavach out and HBL is a Rs 1,600 cr battery and defence business earning about Rs 400 cr of segment profit, growing slowly, worth a fraction of Rs 22,400 cr. The new businesses have missed every dated milestone since 2022. The family controls the vote through a trust one director casts, public institutions vote overwhelmingly against its related-party and appointment resolutions, which mostly pass on the trust's votes, the chairman's commission has trebled to Rs 56 cr, and surplus cash is going into associates and a fund. The finance function presents associates' profit on a basis its own notes contradict, and filed its best quarter in a format the exchange queried. The company has never held a quarterly earnings call. For a holder, the exit signal is any forfeiture on the 2026 orders or an FY27 that does not beat FY26.

When to act: the Jan-May 2027 delivery deadlines and the FY27 results (May-2027) are the tests.

The one line

This is a bet on Kavach being a decade-long national programme in which HBL keeps a leading share at today's margins while a second business reaches revenue, priced as if FY26's peak profit is a floor that grows about 18% a year for ten years, and it breaks if the 2026 orders are delivered late or at lower margins, or if FY27 fails to beat FY26.

! The company's own Kavach forecast, ten weeks before year end

Core (batteries, Kavach)

Batteries: steady Rs 335-390 cr a quarter for three years, NiCad utilisation 85-90% (CARE), exports up, no announced slippage. New verticals

Verdict: twenty years of development, one small approved fuze, no drivetrain revenue. These belong in an option bucket, not a forecast (Section 8 scores the dates).

Growth strategy as management states it (AR FY26 MD&A)

The platform above HBL

There is no GeM-style intermediary here: the platform above HBL is the Ministry of Railways itself, acting through RDSO (approval), the production units (on-board purchases) and the zonal railways (trackside). That asymmetry is the whole competitive story: one buyer, a published unit price, several approved sellers. The "competition" is not for customers, who are fixed, but for allocation within tenders, which is decided on price once a vendor is approved.

Contradictions between official data and company statements

! 1. Official Kavach spending trails what vendors supply

HBL's standalone FY26 revenue from Railways customers was Rs 1,538.61 cr (Ind AS 115 note; a customer category, which may include some non-Kavach products). Official "funds utilised on Kavach works" rose by only Rs 813.90 cr between the Mar-2025 and Mar-2026 Lok Sabha replies (Rs 1,950 cr "so far" to Rs 2,763.90 cr to Feb-2026), then by Rs 1,111 cr in the four months to Jun-2026. Over roughly the same period locomotives fitted rose from 795 to 4,154, about Rs 2,700 cr of equipment at the ministry's Rs 80 lakh a set. The official series therefore lags supply, or leaves out central on-board procurement through CLW, or both; which one is not disclosed (the budget head for CLW's Kavach purchases would settle it). The practical point: official Kavach spend is not a measure of, or a ceiling on, vendor revenue, and a reader should not size HBL's Kavach business from the Kavach budget line.

! 2. "No other Indian company" in electronic fuzes

HBL (AR FY26): "To our knowledge there is no other Indian company with this capability." ECIL states it has been "primary supplier of Electronic Fuzes to the Indian Army for the past 25 years, delivering over 2.2 million units", and Bharat Electronics holds a ten-year electronic-fuze contract. HBL's claim is defensible only in its narrowest reading (every component made in-house); as a statement about competition it is wrong.

! 3. The FY29 decline is not a demand cliff

HBL's MD&A says Kavach "sales and PBT could decline from FY 29" as competition rises (the 2025 AGM said FY28). The official scope sanctioned but unspent (about Rs 17,800 cr) lasts five to nine years at recent spending rates. The official record therefore supports management's stated reason (competition) and contradicts any reading that the market itself runs out by FY29. It also means the decline is within the company's control only through price and execution.

  • Input costs (consistent): the MD&A is silent on lead, nickel and cadmium; the measured series were benign in dollars through FY26 (lead -7.9%, nickel -18.6% against FY24) and turned up in FY27 (nickel +12.5%). Wholesale basic-metal prices rose 10.9% year on year in Aug-2026 (WPI, new base) with fuel and power up 22.9%: the cost headwind for FY27 is energy and the rupee, consistent with the MD&A's West Asia and shipping comments.
  • E-trucks (consistent): the ministry's own description of the e-truck market as "nascent", with nil incentives paid, agrees with HBL's slipped timetable.
  • Kavach demand "flood" (largely supported): the 2025 AGM claim that a "drought of Kavach orders has now become a flood" is borne out by awards and allocation, with two cautions: trackside scope "taken up" fell from 24,427 to 21,794 route km between the Mar and Aug-2026 statements, and FY26 allocation use was low.
