Aeroflex Industries

NSE: AEROFLEX · BSE: 543972 · 18-Nov-2026 · Data as of Q2 FY27 (quarter ended 30-Sep-2026) · Price ₹494.60 (18-Nov-2026 close)

A Mumbai-area maker of stainless-steel flexible hose, a sick company until 2018 and an exporter for most of its life, that in one year became the exclusive India supplier of liquid-cooling skids to a large US data-centre equipment company. Skids are now a quarter of revenue and most of the reason the stock trades near 80 times earnings. The question this report tries to answer is how much of the growth is the customer's, how much of the profit growth is accounting, and what the price already assumes.

Executive summary

Aeroflex is two businesses on one balance sheet. One is a long-established stainless-steel hose exporter with a low-cost Indian base, 42-44% gross margins and growth of 15-18% a year, whose exports grew only 6% in FY26 before recovering in H1 FY27. The other, a year old, makes liquid-cooling skids for one US data-centre equipment company under a five-year India exclusivity: 1,475 units and Rs 50 cr in Q2 FY27, a quarter of revenue. The stock, at about 79 times trailing profit on a consistent basis, is priced for the second business to keep compounding. Reported profit growth runs ahead of both, because of exchange gains booked in operating revenue and a change of depreciation method booked in one quarter.

The three strongest positives

  1. The skid business is real and ahead of its own guidance. 46, 571, 1,040 and 1,475 units in four quarters, Rs 101 cr of revenue in the first three full quarters. No skid margin has been disclosed; the company's overall margin rose as skids grew. The customer designs with Aeroflex and gave it five years of India exclusivity; international supply is at the pricing stage. Capacity goes to 15,000 a year in Dec-2026 and 30,000 by Mar-2028 (Sections 2, 18).
  2. A low-cost, export-qualified hose franchise underneath. About 29% of India's exports of the product class, 3,274 SKUs, 60-66% of exports to the Americas, an EBITDA margin of about 23% against 11.2% at the operating line for the closest global comparable, Senior Flexonics (different measures, same direction). Revenue excluding skids grew 42% in Q2 FY27 as US orders resumed (Sections 3, 4, 5).
  3. No debt and no pledge. Borrowings under Rs 1 cr; the promoter has never pledged and has only been diluted. Management has delivered its EBITDA growth and margin guidance every year since listing, even while missing almost every new-product date (Sections 7, 8).

The three sharpest risks

  1. The price assumes the plan, and the plan is one customer. 79x trailing profit, twice the global suppliers' multiples, needs profit to compound about 29% a year to FY31. Firm skid visibility is one quarter; the contract fixes exclusivity, not volumes; capacity is being built to the customer's forecast. New-vertical promises have been met 25% of the time (Sections 8, 14, 18).
  2. Earnings quality is low and moving the flattering way. Rs 15.03 cr of FY26 exchange gains sit in operating revenue; a depreciation change adds about Rs 17 cr a year to pre-tax profit and was booked in one quarter, with its tax effect misstated in the note; EBITDA was redefined. Export bills overdue for realisation were 31-44% of standalone receivables in FY24-FY26 (Sections 5, 6).
  3. Disclosure and governance gaps. The accounts say the customer base is widely distributed, while the MD puts the largest international customer at 25-26% of "our sales". Sales to a promoter-group UAE trader rose eight times with no balances shown. A Rs 55 cr preferential issue was described with different objects in two filings of the same day (capex in the board outcome, working capital in the EOGM notice), which the MD later reconciled only on a call. There are Rs 51 cr of disclosed tax claims, including Rs 41.76 cr on the 2018 bank settlement of a once-sick company. The company has had no credit rating since 2023 (Sections 6, 7, 9).
The one line

This is a bet that one global data-centre customer keeps scaling Aeroflex's skids from India and then from India to the world, priced as if profit grows about 29% a year for five years to a 40x exit, and it breaks if skid volumes stall near 1,500 a quarter once the Dec-2026 capacity is installed.

Industry and sector analysis

Aeroflex sells into two industries with different clocks. Stainless-steel flexible hose, assemblies and metal bellows is a mature global industry growing 4-9% a year, where the scaled European incumbents are flat or shrinking and an Indian exporter wins by cost and breadth. Data-centre liquid cooling is a young market growing 20-50% a year on any publisher's numbers, where the value sits with a few global thermal-management platforms and a component maker is a qualified subcontractor. No official series measures either market in India; every size below is commissioned research or a company deck, and is labelled so.

Where India sits. India is a small but fast-growing exporter: HS 8307 exports grew 15.6% a year over 2021-2025 while the two scaled incumbents went nowhere. Senior plc's Flexonics division grew revenue 0.1% at constant currency in FY25 (GBP 313.4m, 11.2% adjusted operating margin), and Senior agreed in Apr-2026 to be taken private by a Tinicum and Blackstone vehicle. Witzenmann, the German family-owned leader, reported turnover down from EUR 740m to EUR 706m in 2025 and opened cost-saving talks with its works council (notes-06 A2, A3). Management calls Aeroflex the largest exporter of hoses and assemblies from India "by far" (Q1FY26 call). The arithmetic above makes the claim plausible in order of magnitude (about 29% of the HS 8307 line) but does not prove it, because other exporters are unnamed.

Cycle position: mid-cycle for the industry, with Aeroflex carrying spare capacity. Evidence: (1) volume - the global incumbents are flat to down, while India's export line grew 15.1% in 2025 and kept growing into the US even after the 50% tariff arrived in Aug-2025 (Comtrade, calendar basis not confirmed); (2) utilisation - Aeroflex's own hose utilisation was 70% in FY26 and 65-66% in Jul-2026, rising to "about 80%" by 29-Oct-2026 on a larger 20 mn metre base (Q1FY27 call p4; Q2FY27 call p15), against 90% in FY22, so the company is adding capacity (17.5 to 20 to 30 mn m) ahead of current demand; (3) pricing - the tariff shock forced an 8% price concession to US customers by Q3FY26 (analyst figure accepted by the MD, Q3FY26 p17); (4) inventory across the chain - nothing verified; no distributor stock data exist in the record. Aeroflex's own exports grew only 6.2% in FY26 (AR FY26 note 21, derived), in line with the 6.8-7.2% global market, then accelerated to about +46% in H1FY27 (derived from deck mix; management credits ports and terminals). One strong half-year does not yet make an upcycle.

Cycle position: early upcycle, demand real but pipeline overstated. Measured Indian data-centre additions in H1 2026 were 85-130 MW (opened reports), and JLL reports absorption of 101 MW, 20% above its three-year H1 average. Announced pipelines need two to seven times that pace (Section 20). Liquid cooling is a step change inside that build: AI racks need direct-to-chip cooling, and the secondary fluid network (SFN) skids that Aeroflex fabricates carry coolant from the coolant distribution unit to the racks. India's first large deployment went live only in Oct-2026, so the market is at the start of its curve, and a supplier's volumes depend on one or two platform vendors' India programmes rather than on industry-wide demand.

3. Structural shifts

  • Consolidation and retreat of the incumbents. Senior is going private; Witzenmann is cutting cost; both still market data-centre products. A weaker incumbent base is an opening for a low-cost exporter, but also a source of aggressive pricing from private-equity owners (inference).
  • From hose to assembly. Value is moving from metres of hose to engineered, end-fitted assemblies and complete fluid modules. Aeroflex's "assemblies and others" share rose from about 33% (Q4FY24 call) to 41% of Q2FY27 revenue, plus 25% skids. Part of that rise is definitional (Section 2).
  • Liquid cooling as a new demand pool for stainless fluid conveyance. Witzenmann, Hose Master and Gates all now market data-centre hose; Vertiv has built its own skid production facility in India (secondary). The shift is real; who captures it is open.
  • Tariffs and China+1. The 2025-2026 US tariff sequence penalised Indian supply directly. Whether Indian hose gains share from Chinese hose under the same regime is not evidenced in the record (nothing verified on Chinese HS 8307 flows).
  • Import dependence on inputs. India is "100% import-dependent on nickel" (NITI Trade Watch Q1FY27), and Aeroflex is "still predominantly importing" stainless coil, "Most of it is from China" (Q3FY25 p15). Formalisation and import substitution in the input, not the output, is the live question.
  • Disruption risk: composite and polymer hose substitutes in low-pressure uses; in data centres, a platform vendor in-sourcing skids (Vertiv's India facility is the live example).

Input-side regulation cuts against an importer

The two Indian measures that touch Aeroflex most are both on its input, not its output. A quality control order on stainless flats and an anti-dumping case on cold-rolled imports (both secondary, unverified) would raise the cost or narrow the source of the Chinese coil that management says it mostly uses. If the QCO exemption lapses on 31-Mar-2027 as reported, the effect on Aeroflex's coil cost is not quantifiable from the record. It belongs on the monitoring list rather than in the base case.

Business model and value chain

Aeroflex is a Maharashtra maker of stainless-steel flexible hoses, founded as a converter and rescued from sickness: it was referred to BIFR in 2010, taken over by Sat Industries (now Aeroflex Enterprises) through a bank one-time settlement in 2018, and listed in Aug-2023. It earns money three ways. It corrugates and braids imported stainless strip into hose and sells most of it abroad; it turns that hose, plus fittings, bellows and composite hose, into finished assemblies; and since Dec-2025 it fabricates liquid-cooling skids for one US multinational's data-centre programme in India. Exports were 69% of FY26 sales and 60% of Q2FY27, falling because the skids are entirely domestic.

Unknown unknowns

SS hoses: dependencies a first read would miss
  • A large single customer the accounts do not show. Asked about "the U.S. business ex of skid assemblies", the MD said: "our biggest customer in the international market contributes about 25% to 26% of our sales right now" (Q4FY26 call p11, 06-May-2026). The denominator is ambiguous: about 25% of revenue if he meant total sales, about 17-18% if he meant international sales. Either reading is above the 10% threshold in Ind AS 108 para 34; only a US-sales reading (about 8.6% of revenue) falls below it. AR FY24 to FY26 carry identical wording, "no concentration of credit risk as the customer base is widely distributed", and no major-customer disclosure. The prospectus gave top-5 customers at 24.1% of FY23 revenue.
  • Chinese coil. The dominant input is imported, mostly from China, and the share was never brought below 50% as promised (Q2FY24 p10). An Indian QCO or anti-dumping action on stainless flats would hit cost, not demand (Section 1).
  • Unhedged currency. Forex gains sit inside revenue (Rs 15.03 cr in FY26, all of the year's margin expansion); the company does not hedge (notes-03 section 3).
  • US trade law. Whether an assembly ships under 8307.10.30 or 8307.10.60 decides whether it was a Section 232 derivative from Aug-2025. Classification, not product, sets the duty.
Assemblies and others: dependencies a first read would miss
  • A promoter-owned trader inside the chain. Sales to Italica Global FZC (Ajman free zone; owned by the parent; MD holds its shares as nominee) rose 8x to Rs 22.56 cr in FY26, and Aeroflex began paying Italica a business-development fee. No year-end related-party balances are disclosed (notes-05 F2).
  • Captive subsidiary economics. Hyd-Air shows Rs 0.11 cr PBT on Rs 31.62 cr revenue and no unrealised-profit elimination on intra-group stock (notes-03 section 9). Margin can sit in either entity until the merger closes.
  • Bellows promises. Every bellows revenue target missed by 70-90% (notes-04 F6); the plant is partly being used for data-centre parts, so bellows revenue and skid-related revenue are hard to separate.
SFN skids: dependencies a first read would miss
  • Exclusivity binds Aeroflex too. The five-year India agreement makes Aeroflex the customer's sole Indian skid supplier; it does not commit the customer to volumes, and the start date is undisclosed.
  • The principal can make its own. Vertiv was reported (secondary, 12-Oct-2026) to have set up a production facility in India to mass-manufacture loop skids. If the customer is Vertiv (inference), Aeroflex competes with its own customer's plant.
  • No IP. The design is joint and the principal holds it; Aeroflex has no patent on the skid.
  • Pass-through content. Valves, connectors and fittings are bought in. The parent has reportedly acquired a valves business (analyst assertion on the Q2FY27 call, unverified), which would create a related-party channel into the fastest-growing line if it ever supplies the skids.
  • Single geography. All skids ship to Indian data centres; one country's data-centre build rate and one platform's India share drive 25% of revenue.
  • Capex on a forecast. The 2,000 to 15,000 to 30,000 capacity plan was sized "based on their projections at that time" (Q1FY27 call); the FY27 utilisation outlook has already fallen from 60-70% to 40-50%.
Company-wide
  • One state. Taloja, Palava and Chakan are all in Maharashtra; there is no plant elsewhere.
  • No rating, no bank lines. With no external credit rating since Oct-2023 and no working-capital limit above Rs 5 cr (CARO), no lender monitors stock and debtors (Section 9).
  • Control and economics diverge. The promoter family's look-through economic interest is about 34% of Aeroflex but about 52% of Italica (notes-05 F2, inference from shareholdings).

Competitive advantage: the 6 Cs

Aeroflex's durable edge is an Indian cost base applied to a broad, export-qualified catalogue of stainless hose and assemblies: it earns about twice the operating margin of the European incumbent on similar products and sells into the US and Europe through distributors it has served for years. The liquid-cooling business rests on something narrower: a five-year India exclusivity with one platform customer that owns the design. The market is valuing the narrow advantage at least as highly as the durable one.

Cost Moderate

The advantage is location and labour, not inputs. FY26 EBITDA of 22.57% against Senior Flexonics' 11.2% adjusted operating margin is the evidence that an Indian converter undercuts European ones. But there is no input edge: coil is mostly imported from China, nickel is fully imported, and the promise to cut imports below 50% was missed (Q3FY25 p15). Excluding forex gains booked in revenue, the FY26 margin was flat at about 19.8% (notes-03 section 3). The 8% price concession to US customers in FY26 shows the cost edge absorbing tariffs rather than widening.

Capacity Moderate

Headroom exists: hose utilisation 65% in Q2FY27 on 20 mn m, going to 30 mn m by Mar-2028 (Rs 124 cr); skids 12,000 a year going to 30,000 (Rs 78 cr). That is optionality, not a barrier. Capacity has been added ahead of demand in hose and on a customer forecast in skids; FY27 skid utilisation guidance already fell from 60-70% to 40-50%. Time to replicate is short: a skid line took Aeroflex about a year from first discussion to first shipment (Q1FY27 call), and the probable customer has built its own Indian skid facility (secondary). Capacity promises slip by one to six quarters as a rule (notes-04 Part 1).

Capital Weak

Net cash is thin: cash and bank Rs 28.46 cr and investments Rs 4.01 cr at 30-Sep-2026 against borrowings Rs 0.85 cr and lease liabilities Rs 23.14 cr (notes-01 section 3). Bank balances fell from Rs 50.66 cr to Rs 3.66 cr in six months while receivables rose Rs 74 cr. There is no credit rating since Oct-2023 and no bank working-capital line above Rs 5 cr. The Rs 202 cr expansion is to be funded by "internal accruals, bridge loan financing and equity fund raise if required" (Q2FY27 p4), nine months after "we don't plan to take on any debt". The parent is cash-rich after selling M.R. Organisation (Rs 227.42 cr, secondary) but has made no commitment to fund Aeroflex.

Channel Moderate

A working export channel: Americas 66% and Europe 25% of Q2FY27 exports, US distributors (pipe-joining and hose names in shipment data, lower reliability) and OEMs, and 3,274 SKUs to cover a distributor's range. The weakness is concentration and ownership: the largest international customer is "about 25% to 26% of our sales" in the MD's words (Q4FY26 p11; about 17-25% of revenue depending on the denominator, inference), the distributor owns the end customer, and a promoter-owned UAE trader (Italica) took Rs 22.56 cr in FY26 and is now paid a business-development fee. No evidence of channel stuffing in the record, but receivables over one year past due nearly tripled to Rs 10.61 cr in FY26.

Customer captivity Moderate

In skids, switching costs are real but run both ways: joint design, end-customer audits that delayed Q4FY26 shipments (Q4FY26 call), and a five-year India exclusivity. The principal holds the design and the IP, commits no volumes, and gives about two months of firm schedule. In hose, repeat distributor business and SKU breadth create stickiness; approvals and certifications that would lock customers in are not documented in the record. Concentration is high and under-disclosed: the largest hose customer is about 17-25% of revenue depending on how the MD's "25% to 26% of our sales" is read, and from FY27 the single skid customer is about a quarter of revenue (24.1% in Q2FY27), while the annual report says the base is "widely distributed". The skid point applies to FY27 reporting; skids were 5% of FY26 revenue.

Compliance moat Weak

One patent granted, for automated 3D CAD modelling of braided hose assemblies (Q2FY27 p4); none on the skid. The deck cites an NABL-accredited R&D lab and 90+ R&D staff (placement on the slide ambiguous). Product approvals by end-market (fire-safety listings, pressure-equipment certificates) are not set out in the notes, so the strength of this moat is not documented: the report rates it on what is shown, and records the gap. "Approved supplier" status with the skid customer is the one qualification that clearly matters.

The most important moat, and its half-life

The low-cost, export-qualified hose and assembly franchise. It is the business that took a sick company to Rs 442 cr of revenue at 20-23% margins, holds about 29% of India's HS 8307 exports on the report's arithmetic (inference), and outgrew Senior Flexonics and Witzenmann while both stalled. Its half-life is long, perhaps seven to ten years, eroded by tariffs, Chinese coil cost and customer concentration rather than by a better competitor (judgement). The skid advantage has a short and dated half-life: the exclusivity runs five years from an undisclosed start (about 2025-2026 on the call record), the customer owns the design, and the customer may already make the same product in India. On the evidence, it is a two-to-four-year advantage that has to be renewed by execution, not a moat. The valuation in Section 14 should be read against that difference.

Durability badges are the report's judgement on the evidence cited; the Ratings control hides them.

The business under the numbers is real and growing: excluding exchange gains, FY26 EBITDA rose 16.6%, and cash from operations has covered 80% of profit over four years. The depreciation switch, as a quality item

Straight-line from 01-Apr-2026: permitted, large, and stated wrongly in the results note

Section 5 sets out the mechanics. For earnings quality, four points matter. First, size: the change lowered H1 FY27 depreciation by Rs 8.41 cr, all booked in Q2 (charge Rs 0.45 cr), and annualises to about Rs 17 cr of pre-tax profit, 23% of FY26 PBT; the gap widens as the Rs 202 cr Phase-2 capex is capitalised. Second, it rests on a 25-year life for plant and machinery, longer than the 15 years Schedule II indicates for general plant, with no technical evaluation disclosed. Third, the results note misstates the after-tax effect as Rs 8.23 cr (a 2% tax rate; the standalone figure is larger than its own pre-tax effect). The true effect at 25.168% is Rs 6.30 cr. The P&L books broadly the right deferred tax, so reported profit is not overstated by the note's error. Fourth, the growth it adds is the smaller part: on the old method, profit was Rs 30.26 cr in Q2 FY27 (+113% against +157% reported), Rs 49.06 cr in H1 (+129% against +159%) and Rs 83.18 cr over the trailing twelve months. The auditors' limited reviews for Q2 FY27 do carry an emphasis-type paragraph on the change, unlabelled, ending "Our conclusion has not been modified in respect of this matter" (the consolidated paragraph gives Q2 depreciation as Rs 44.17 lakh against Rs 44.76 lakh in the P&L, a typo). The CFO handover approved at the same 28-Oct-2026 meeting is the scheduled end of a two-year term that began 28-Nov-2024: a coincidence of timing, with no evidence of a link. The FY26 AR, authorised 05-May-2026, says nothing about the coming change.

Promoter, governance and capital allocation

Aeroflex has no individual promoter. It is controlled by a listed parent, Aeroflex Enterprises (formerly Sat Industries), which bought a sick, wilful-defaulter-listed company for about Rs 20.5 cr in 2018-2019, took out Rs 265 cr in 2023 through pre-IPO sales and the offer for sale, and has not bought a share since. Since the IPO the record is a mix: low pay, no pledge and a modest dividend on one side; on the other, a preferential issue whose use of money was described two ways on the same day, an 8x jump in sales to a parent-owned UAE trader with no balances disclosed, a five-month COO, and two of four independent directors who also sit on the parent's board.

History: from BIFR to a takeover at Rs 1.95 a share

The company was referred to BIFR as sick on 14-Jul-2010. SBI took possession of its assets, lenders ran SARFAESI and DRT proceedings, and the company sat on the CIBIL wilful-defaulter list (prospectus p.268-269). Sat Industries took control on 02-Apr-2018; lenders settled by compromise or one-time settlement (OTS), or assigned their debt to IARC and Edelweiss ARC; no-dues letters run from Mar-2018 to Sep-2018, and the CIBIL status was cleared only by 05-Jun-2023, under three months before listing. Sat paid about Rs 20.50 cr for its 92.18%: Rs 14.74 cr to former promoter Yusuf Mohammad Kagzi for 67,01,300 shares at Rs 22 (Rs 10 face value) on 22-Oct-2019, and preference positions bought from Avigo Trustee and Park Continental Pvt Ltd (about 91% of the cost; itself a 3.58% holder of the parent's promoter entity Sat Invest) at Rs 3.45 to Rs 80.84 (prospectus p.92-94, p.208-209). Italica's 6.52% came from converting the preference shares originally allotted to Bond Street Custodians; what Italica paid is not disclosed (prospectus p.86-88). The auditor-certified weighted average cost of the shares sold in the IPO was Rs 1.95 a share on the Rs 2 face value. Some RoC records for the 1994 allotment are untraceable (prospectus p.87).

The OTS still has a cost. An income-tax reassessment order of 25-Mar-2026 for AY 2018-19 treats the Rs 60.92 cr of waived working-capital interest as income and raises a demand of Rs 41.76 cr, with s.270A penalty proceedings initiated; the company has appealed (Reg 30, 27-Mar-2026, filed late, which the company attributes to festival leave). A separate s.74 CGST order of Rs 7.19 cr (tax plus equal penalty) disallows input credit on IPO expenses (Reg 30, 08-Apr-2026). Neither is provided for (Section 9). The legal case against the income-tax demand is reasonable: interest never allowed as a deduction under s.43B is arguably outside s.41(1), and most no-dues letters fall in AY 2019-20, not AY 2018-19. But the cost of a cheap 2018 entry is now a contingent claim on every shareholder, including IPO and preferential investors who paid 55 to 94 times the parent's entry price.

Valuation

At Rs 494.60 (NSE close, 18-Nov-2026) Aeroflex is priced at Rs 6,545 cr: about 79 times trailing profit measured on one consistent depreciation basis, and 45 times trailing EBITDA. Both sit at the 82nd percentile of the stock's own post-listing range and roughly twice the multiples of the global thermal-management and fluid-handling companies in its industry. The price already assumes profit compounding at 29-37% a year for five years and a premium exit multiple at the end of it.

The right multiple

Price to earnings on a trailing base that uses one depreciation method is the primary measure. The company is net cash (about Rs 8.5 cr after leases), so EV and market value barely differ, and the business is a manufacturer whose capex runs ahead of depreciation, which makes EV/EBITDA flatter it. The reported trailing P/E of 73-74 (screener) is not usable as it stands: it adds the Q2 FY27 depreciation catch-up to three quarters on the old method. The base used here is trailing profit of Rs 83.2 cr, with H1 FY27 restated to the old written-down-value method (Rs 49.06 cr; Section 5) plus H2 FY26 as reported (Rs 34.13 cr). On that base the P/E is 78.7. On straight-line depreciation throughout, a fairer forward-looking base, trailing profit would be about Rs 93-94 cr and the P/E about 70.

Pre-mortem and anti-thesis

Assume it is Nov-2028 and the stock has halved. This is the most likely story of how that happened, argued as a short-seller would argue it.

The short case, argued sincerely

  1. One customer is the whole growth story, and it holds every card. Skids went from nothing to 24% of revenue in four quarters, all sold to one US multinational on a five-year India exclusivity that, on management's own description, fixes who supplies, not how much (Section 18). Firm visibility is about one quarter. The capex for 30,000 skids a year was sized on the customer's forecast. When that forecast is cut, or a second Indian source is qualified, the new plant sits idle and its straight-line depreciation does not.
  2. The core business was flat before skids arrived, and is now running below capacity. Exports grew 6% in FY26. Hose utilisation fell from 88% (FY24) to 65-66% (Jul-2026) while capacity went from 13.5 to 20 mn metres; it recovered to about 80% in Oct-2026 on one strong half-year, and a further Rs 124 cr is to take capacity to 30 mn. US tariffs on the main export market are unresolved. Strip out forex and FY26 operating margin did not move.
  3. The profit line was helped three ways in eighteen months. Rs 15 cr of exchange gains booked as operating revenue in FY26; a change of depreciation method worth about Rs 17 cr a year of pre-tax profit, booked entirely in one quarter, announced at the same board meeting that named a new CFO (the outgoing CFO's two-year term was ending, so the timing may be routine); an EBITDA definition that widened the FY26 beat. None was hidden, and none was explained on a call.
  4. The disclosures do not match what management says. The annual report says there is no concentration of credit risk, the customer base being widely distributed; the MD says "our biggest customer in the international market contributes about 25% to 26% of our sales", and skids, one customer, are 24% of the latest quarter. A related party in the UAE, held by the MD as nominee for the promoter group, bought eight times more in FY26 and was paid a new fee, with no year-end balances shown. Export receivables overdue for realisation, with no extension sought, were 31-44% of the standalone receivables book in FY24-FY26, far more than the ageing schedule shows as six months past due.
  5. Management's promises on new things have a 25% hit rate.   6. The history is a sick company. BIFR from 2010, SARFAESI action and a wilful-defaulter listing before the 2018 settlement whose interest waiver is now the subject of a Rs 41.76 cr tax demand. The company has had no credit rating since Oct-2023, a Rs 3 lakh statutory audit fee, lost a COO after five months and a company secretary in 2025, and names a new CFO from Nov-2026 as the outgoing one's term ends.

Base rates

Indian small-cap component suppliers re-rated on one large new customer in a hot end-market have a poor record once that customer's ordering normalises: the pattern of a capacity build sized on the customer's forecast, a peak multiple set during the ramp, and then a pricing reset at the first renegotiation is familiar from Indian EMS, solar-module and telecom-equipment suppliers. The base rate for companies trading near 80 times earnings delivering the 29% compound growth the price needs for five years is low; most de-rate even when they grow. Aeroflex's own record adds a specific reason for caution: its revenue promises for new verticals are met a quarter of the time. The counterweight is real: the skid business so far has beaten its own first revenue guidance, because the customer, not management, sets the schedule.

Investment pitch: both sides

No rating and no target. Two cases, each argued as its advocate would argue it to an investment committee, and one line that states the bet.

The 90-second case to own it

Aeroflex makes stainless-steel flexible hose, about 29% of India's exports of the product class, and sells 60-70% of it abroad at 42-44% gross margins with no debt. In 2025 it became the exclusive India supplier of liquid-cooling skids to a large US thermal-management company, and the business went from 46 units to 1,475 a quarter in a year: Rs 50 cr of revenue in Q2 FY27, a quarter of the company, already beating its first guidance. Data centres in India are being built at hundreds of megawatts a year, and every high-density rack needs liquid cooling. Capacity goes to 15,000 skids by Dec-2026 and 30,000 by Mar-2028, and talks for international supply to the same customer are at the pricing stage. Meanwhile the core business recovered: revenue excluding skids grew 42% in Q2 FY27. Management guides Rs 715-725 cr for FY27 (+60%) and about Rs 1,500 cr at 25% EBITDA by FY30, which supports the price at a 40x exit. A supplier that a global leader has chosen, designed with and given exclusivity to does not get replaced quickly, and the first skid quarters show what that relationship earns.

Horizon: 2-3 years, through FY28, with the first international skid order and two quarters above 2,500 units as the proof points.

The 90-second case to exit or avoid

At 79 times trailing earnings on a consistent basis, about twice the multiples of Vertiv and Parker, the price already needs management's FY30 plan delivered on time. That plan rests on one customer with one quarter of firm visibility, built on capacity sized to that customer's forecast, by a management whose new-vertical promises have come true a quarter of the time. Exports grew 6% in FY26, and hose capacity is being lifted another 50% on the strength of one good half-year. Profit growth has been helped by forex in operating revenue, a depreciation change booked in one quarter, and a redefined EBITDA. The annual report says the customer base is widely distributed while the MD describes a customer at about a quarter of sales, does not show balances with a promoter-group trading company whose purchases rose eight times, and reports a third or more of its receivables as export bills overdue for realisation. Institutions have left; the remaining float is retail and preferential allottees sitting on a 2.7x gain.

When to act: the Q3 FY27 result (late Jan-2027), the first quarter with 15,000 skid capacity installed, is the test.

The one line

This is a bet that one global data-centre customer keeps scaling Aeroflex's skids from India and then from India to the world, priced as if profit grows about 29% a year for five years to a 40x exit, and it breaks if skid volumes stall near 1,500 a quarter once the Dec-2026 capacity is installed.

The platform above Aeroflex

In skids, the layer above Aeroflex is the thermal-management platform that designs the secondary fluid network, owns the data-centre operator relationship and specifies the skid. Management names Vertiv as the industry leader in India and says Vertiv's design team works with Aeroflex's on the SFN design; Vertiv as the customer is an inference, not a disclosure. On that inference, the scale gap is the whole story. Vertiv's trailing revenue of USD 13.12bn is about Rs 1,16,000 cr at Rs 88.4 per USD, roughly 260 times Aeroflex's FY26 revenue and about 700 times its annualised H1FY27 skid revenue (inference). Its market value is USD 73.30bn against Aeroflex's Rs 6,545 cr. Its EBITDA margin (24.31%) is about the same as Aeroflex's, earned on the system rather than the fabrication.

Two facts reframe "competition" as a question of how long the platform chooses to buy. First, Vertiv, the probable customer on the report's inference, reported (secondary, 12-Oct-2026) that it set up its own Indian production facility to mass-manufacture loop skids for its first 30 MW deployment. Second, the platform holds the design and the IP; Aeroflex has no patent on the skid "since they are the principal". The skid business is therefore a share of a platform's make-or-buy decision, and that platform's India output is not disclosed anywhere in the record. In hose, the layer above is the US distributor, which owns the end customer; it is fragmented, so no single platform is sized.

Chapter 9 (para 9.47): about 1,280 MW in June 2025 across about 130 private and 49 government data centres, rising to about 4 GW by 2030 ("industry estimates"). Same document, same date (29-Jan-2026), a twofold gap in the 2030 figure. Any skid market sized on the official projection is uncertain by a factor of two before it starts. ! 2. Management's market sizes disagree with its own deck

Asked for the India market size, the MD said "exactly the numbers are not available" (Q2FY27 call). The company has no stable figure for the market it is building capacity for.

! 3. Measured additions trail announced pipelines, and Aeroflex's implied skid megawatts are large against them

JLL's path to 6 GW by 2029 needs about 1.26 GW a year against about 170 MW annualised in H1 2026, roughly 7x; CBRE's path to 3 GW by end-2028 needs about 0.5 GW a year against about 260 MW annualised, roughly 2x (notes-06 B2, derived). Against that measured base, Aeroflex's own numbers imply a lot of liquid cooling. At "about 40-odd skids" per megawatt (Q1FY27 call), the 2,515 skids shipped in H1FY27 correspond to about 63 MW, and the FY27 skid guide of Rs 150-200 cr at Rs 3.39 lakh a skid to about 110-150 MW (inference). That is the same order as India's entire measured data-centre additions in H1 2026 (85-130 MW, all cooling types), while the first large direct-to-chip deployment in India (30 MW) went live only in Oct-2026. The numbers can be reconciled if skids are built months ahead of commissioning, if the skids-per-MW ratio is loose, or if skids serve capacity under construction that the property consultants have not yet counted; the record does not say which. It is the single most useful question to put to management on skid demand.
